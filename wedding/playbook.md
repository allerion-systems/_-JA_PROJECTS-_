# The 154-Guest Wedding Playbook

You run projects for a living: scope, budget, schedule, subs, punch lists. A wedding is the same shape — a hard-deadline project with ~15–20 vendors and 154 stakeholders — with one difference that changes everything: **you are the client, not the PM.** Most of the mental preparation is learning to act like it.

---

## Part 1 — Getting your head right

### You're the owner, not the superintendent

On a job site you'd never let the owner direct traffic on pour day. On your wedding day, *you're the owner.* Your only deliverables are: show up, be present, get married. Everything else must be executed by someone who is not you and not your spouse. If the budget allows, that's a day-of coordinator (worth every dollar at this headcount). If not, deputize the most organized, least sentimental person you know and hand them the run sheet, the vendor contact list, and full authority to make calls without asking you.

**The test:** if the caterer is 20 minutes late on the day, who gets that phone call? If the answer is "me," you're not done planning.

### Beat decision fatigue with batching

A wedding generates hundreds of small decisions, and decision fatigue — not cost — is what burns most couples out. Handle it like submittals:

- **One planning meeting a week**, time-boxed. Decisions get made in the meeting, not in a running 11pm text thread.
- **Two-way door rule:** anything reversible (napkin color, font, favor) gets decided in under 5 minutes and never revisited. Save deliberation for one-way doors: venue, headcount, photographer, band vs. DJ.
- **Divide and own:** split the vendor list between you two. The owner of a lane makes the call in that lane; the other gets a veto they're allowed to use maybe twice total.

### The 90/10 rule

Something will go wrong. At 154 guests and 20 vendors, the probability of a zero-defect day is effectively nil — a boutonnière will wilt, a toast will run long, a table number will be missing. Here is the thing nobody tells you: **the guests will not see any of it.** They will remember whether the food was hot, whether the music filled the floor, and whether you two looked happy. That's the whole punch list. Decide *now*, months out, that the day-of defects are part of the story you'll tell later. Couples consistently report the same thing afterward: the mishap became the best anecdote.

### The math of 154 relationships

You cannot have a real conversation with 154 people in one evening — don't set yourself up to fail at it. Pick one strategy and schedule it:

- **Table visits during dinner:** ~16–20 tables × 2–3 minutes = 45–60 minutes. Doable if you start as soon as you've eaten (and you *will* eat — see below).
- **Receiving line after the ceremony:** 154 people at ~15–20 seconds each ≈ 40–50 minutes. Eats most of cocktail hour; only choose this if photos are done pre-ceremony.
- **Cocktail-hour circulation** (requires a first look so formals are done early).

Everyone you miss, you catch on the dance floor or at brunch the next day. Release yourself from the rest.

### Day-of non-negotiables

1. **Eat breakfast, and eat at the reception.** Have the caterer plate and *hold* two meals for you. Couples who "didn't get to eat at their own wedding" all skipped this step.
2. **Water between drinks.** You're on your feet for 12+ hours in front of every camera you know.
3. **Take 5 minutes alone together** immediately after the ceremony (some couples formalize this — a "yichud"-style private room). It's the only quiet moment you'll get, and it's consistently the one couples say they remember most.
4. **Build one memory on purpose.** At some point during dinner or the first dance, stop, look at the full room, and take a mental photograph. The day moves at time-lapse speed; this is the frame you keep.
5. **Phone off, in someone else's pocket, from hair-and-makeup onward.**

### The weeks before

- Expect a wave of family opinions as the day approaches. The answer to almost all of them is a warm "we've got it handled." You are not obligated to relitigate closed decisions.
- Nerves in the final week are normal and are about the *event*, not the marriage. Cold feet about logistics ≠ cold feet.
- Sleep is a line item. The rehearsal-dinner afterparty is not worth a wedding-day hangover.

## Part 2 — The numbers at 154 guests

*(Ranges are typical US figures — verify everything against your actual quotes.)*

### Headcount chain

| Stage | Number | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Invited | 154 (if this is your invite list) | Expect **75–85%** acceptance → ~115–130 attending |
| Invited | ~180–185 | If you want **154 in the room**, invite this many |
| Ceremony chairs | attending + 6 | Officiant, photographers, coordinator need space too |
| Vendor meals | +6–10 | Photographer, video, DJ/band, coordinator — contractually required by most |
| Final count to caterer | due 7–14 days out | This locks your bill; it only ever needs to go *down* before the deadline |

### Reception layout (at 154 seated)

- Rounds of 8 → **20 tables** · Rounds of 10 → **16 tables** (plus head/sweetheart table)
- Space: plan **±12–14 sq ft per guest** for seated dinner + dance floor → roughly a 2,000–2,200 sq ft clear floor minimum; confirm the venue's rated capacity is *seated with dance floor*, not standing.
- Dance floor: ~2.5–3 sq ft per guest expecting ~half dancing at once → ~20×20 ft.

### Stationery

- Households run **55–65% of guest count** → ~85–100 invitation suites. Order **10–15 extra** (mistakes, keepsakes, late adds) — reprints have brutal minimums.
- Save-the-dates: 6–9 months out (send only after venue + date are contractually locked). Invitations: 8–10 weeks out; RSVP deadline 4–5 weeks out so you have real numbers before the caterer's final-count date.

### Bar (5-hour reception, full bar)

Rule of thumb: **one drink per guest per hour** → ~770 servings at 154 guests. Typical split for a mixed crowd:

- Beer ~35% → ~270 servings (≈ 11 cases)
- Wine ~35% → ~270 glasses (≈ 55 bottles at 5 glasses/bottle, ~60/40 white-red in warm months)
- Cocktails/spirits ~30% (≈ 12–14 handles mixed) + a champagne pour if toasting (154 flutes ≈ 26 bottles)
- Always: two water stations, and licensed/insured bartenders — **1 bartender per 50–75 guests → 2–3 minimum** or the line becomes the event.

### Cake

154 servings — or the caterer's trick: a small display cake to cut plus sheet cakes in the kitchen. Nobody has ever noticed. Ever.

### Service timing (so the run sheet is honest)

- Plated dinner for 154: **45–60 minutes** with adequate staff (ask the caterer for their server-to-guest ratio; ~1:12–16 for plated).
- Buffet for 154: **double-sided line ≥ 2 stations** or dinner takes an hour+ just to serve.
- Photo formals: ~3–4 minutes per grouping. 12 groupings ≈ 45 minutes — this is why first looks exist.

## Part 3 — Budget framework

National average spend hovers in the mid-$30Ks for ~115 guests; per-guest reality for a full-service 154-guest wedding typically lands **$250–$400+/guest** all-in depending on market and venue class. Set the number you're comfortable with first, then force-rank the categories — the percentages below are the typical starting allocation (tracker in `budget-worksheet.csv`):

| Category | Typical % | Category | Typical % |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venue + catering + bar | 40–50% | Attire + beauty | 5–8% |
| Photo + video | 10–12% | Stationery | 2–3% |
| Flowers + décor | 8–10% | Cake/desserts | 1–2% |
| Music/entertainment | 5–10% | Transportation | 2–3% |
| Planner/coordinator | 5–10% | Officiant + ceremony | 1–2% |

**Non-negotiable PM items:**

- **Contingency: 8–10%**, held back and not allocated. Weddings have change orders too (weather tent, extra shuttle run, overtime hour).
- **Read every contract for:** cancellation/postponement terms, overtime rates, gratuity/service-fee stacking (a "22% service charge" is often *not* the tip), meal requirements for vendors, and rain-call deadlines.
- **Payment schedule:** build one master sheet of every deposit and balance due date. Final balances cluster in the last 2 weeks — know that number now.
- **Day-of cash:** tips and final payments go in labeled envelopes, handed to your point person the day before.

## Part 4 — Risk register

| # | Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|------|-----------|--------|------------|
| 1 | Hair/makeup runs late (the #1 cause of late ceremonies) | High | High | Start time set by working *backward* from ceremony with 45–60 min buffer; trial run done; largest party first |
| 2 | Outdoor weather | Med | High | Tent/indoor plan priced in advance with a contractual rain-call date; decision owner named |
| 3 | Vendor no-show / failure | Low | High | Contracts with day-of cell numbers; point person holds full vendor list; know each vendor's backup policy |
| 4 | Timeline slip cascades (photos → intros → dinner) | High | Med | Buffers between blocks; coordinator empowered to cut, not compress (drop a photo grouping, not the couple's 5 minutes) |
| 5 | Guest over-service / alcohol incident | Med | High | Licensed bartenders with authority to cut off; shuttle or ride-share plan; bar closes 30 min before end |
| 6 | Family dynamics / seating landmines | Med | Med | Seating chart reviewed by both families' "diplomats"; greeters assigned; divorced-parents photo plan agreed *in advance* |
| 7 | Sound ordinance / hard stop | Med | Med | Venue curfew and dB limits in writing; DJ briefed; last dance scheduled 15 min before hard stop |
| 8 | Kids at an adult-length event | Med | Low | Decide kids policy once, state it on the website, hold the line; kids' meals + activity table if yes |
| 9 | RSVP stragglers block the final count | High | Low | RSVP deadline 4–5 wks out; divide the chase list between families at deadline+2 days |
| 10 | You two, running on fumes | Med | High | Everything in Part 1 |

## Part 5 — The week-of handoff package

By the Monday before, your point person should hold:

1. The run sheet (`day-of-runsheet.md`, filled in) — also sent to every vendor for confirmation
2. Vendor list with cell numbers and arrival times
3. Seating chart + backup copies of place cards/signage
4. Payment/tip envelopes, labeled
5. The emergency kit (safety pins, stain pen, pain reliever, phone chargers, steamer, snacks, sewing kit, super glue, band-aids)
6. Explicit authority: *"Don't ask us. Decide."*

Then your job is done, and the only thing left on your schedule is the best day you've planned yet.
