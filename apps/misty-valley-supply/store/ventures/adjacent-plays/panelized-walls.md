# Shop-Fabricated Wall Panels — The Verdict

**5 September 2026.** *Corrects my earlier read. I expected this to come back
positive. It did not — for selling panels to third parties. There is a version
that works and it is not that one.*

---

## 1 · 🔴 There is already a CFS panel plant on I-65, between Louisville and Bonnieville

**Tri-State Panels, LLC — Shepherdsville, Kentucky.** Load-bearing CFS wall
panels, multiple sheathing options, **and installation.** Run by an operations
manager with ~30 years in load-bearing panels, hired out of Northpoint Building
Systems. Serves fourteen states. Targets exactly the segments a new entrant would
chase — assisted living, hotels, military, student housing, mid-rise, condos.
**And they integrate panels with their own floor and roof trusses** — a bundle a
three-person startup cannot match.

Also in range: **Fastwalls, Knoxville TN** (since 2018). **Great Lakes
Prefabrication, Fort Wayne IN** — manufacturer *and installer*. **FORGE,
Norcross GA** — 152,000 sf plant, 550,000+ sf of panels in year one.

**The corridor is covered.**

## 2 · Break-even is approximately one line running flat out

**Vendor-optimistic throughput** for a small CFS panel line is ~10 panels/day at
~150 sf each — **~1,500 sf/day, ~375,000 sf/yr.**

**Annual fixed cost, single line, leased shop** *(model built from Louisville
industrial rents at $6.62/sf NNN, BLS Louisville construction wage of $29.64/hr,
and real used-equipment prices — replace every line with a quote):*

| | Annual |
|---|---:|
| Rent, 12,000 sf | $102,000 |
| Shop labour, 3.0 FTE burdened | $280,800 |
| Detailer, 0.5 FTE | $50,000 |
| Delegated engineering retainer | $50,000 |
| **Insurance delta** — products/completed ops + WC reclass | $45,000 |
| Equipment depreciation, utilities, software, forklift, misc | $104,700 |
| **TOTAL** | **~$632,500** |

| Contribution margin $/sf | Break-even sf/yr | vs. one line's capacity |
|---|---:|---|
| $1.50 | 421,700 | **112% — impossible** |
| $2.00 | 316,300 | 84% |
| $2.50 | 253,000 | 67% |
| $3.50 | 180,700 | 48% |

> **Below ~$2.00/sf of margin, break-even exceeds what one line can physically
> produce at the vendor's own optimistic number.** At $2.50 you run two-thirds
> capacity **every week of the year with no gaps**, in a soft region. Exceed one
> line and you add ~$300K of fixed cost, resetting everything.

**Equipment alone is $143,000–$260,000** against $175,000 of equity — before
rent, insurance, working capital or a dollar of steel.

**And there is no published market price per square foot for CFS wall panels
anywhere.** The entire margin model rests on a number nobody publishes. **That is
the number to discover, not assume.**

## 3 · The Kentucky legal delta is specific and adverse

**KRS 411.340** protects a distributor only where the product is sold *"in its
original manufactured condition or package."*

***West v. KKI, LLC*, 300 S.W.3d 184 (Ky. App. 2008)** held that **assembling** a
product forfeits middleman protection — **even when assembly follows the
manufacturer's instructions** — because the assembler bore independent
responsibility for the assembly.

> **Panelization converts you from a defendant with a statutory escape hatch into
> the manufacturer with no escape hatch.** You also become a "manufacturer" under
> KRS 411.320. And your CGL will not cover design — contractors professional
> liability is a separate policy at real premiums.

*(Note the cut shop is already partly exposed here — cutting to length is
arguably not "original condition" either. But a cut stud has a narrow defect
universe. A panel has a large one.)*

## 4 · ⚠️ Two regulatory landmines, both unresolved

**Kentucky.** KRS 198B.010(18) defines an industrialized building system as *"any
structure **or component thereof** which is wholly or in substantial part
fabricated in an off-site manufacturing facility."* **On its face a shop-built
wall panel is a "component thereof."** If it applies, 815 KAR 7:130 requires a
Certificate of Acceptability, a QA manual, **model plans approved once per model**,
and third-party inspection with a serialised M-Seal before shipment.

> **Per-model plan approval is fundamentally incompatible with custom
> project-specific panels.** If the answer is yes, this business cannot exist in
> that form.

**Tennessee.** TCA §68-126-303 defines a modular building unit to include a
*"preassembled component unit"*; §68-126-304 bars sale or installation without
the commissioner's insignia. The statutory phrase *"as a finished building"* is
the likely off-ramp for open panels — **but it is unverified.**

**Both are phone calls.** KY DHBC Division of Building Code Enforcement,
Frankfort. TN State Fire Marshal, Modular Building Program. **Make them before
buying anything.**

## 5 · Transport gets worse, not better

**Unfinished panels: 10–20 per trailer. Finished exterior panels with cladding
and windows: no more than 2 per trailer.** A bundle load of loose studs is
thousands of linear feet.

And the constraint that quietly kills the corridor thesis: **Kentucky will not
permit reasonably divisible loads.** A stack of wall panels is divisible.
Therefore flat-shipped panels are capped at **8'6" of panel height** without a
permit you probably cannot get — which covers 8'-0" panels and **nothing in
multifamily or hotel work with 9'+ ceilings.** Standing them in an A-frame adds
~5 ft of deck height and blows the 13'6" limit.

**UNVERIFIED and high-value: confirm with a Kentucky permit service before
committing to any panel height strategy.**

## 6 · I need to correct my own earlier number

I told you the 30–60% labour savings *"belong to panelised assemblies."* That is
too generous and I am walking it back.

**The peer-reviewed number is 7–23% man-hours** — Rener, Karatas & Videan,
*Buildings* 13(1):68 (2022), four identical buildings on one jobsite, traditional
vs. prefabricated, interior wall assemblies. That is the defensible figure.

The 30–75% claims are (a) **whole-schedule or field-duration compression, not
man-hours**, (b) measured against scaffold-dependent exterior stick-building
where the comparison is **peak crew headcount on site**, and (c) almost always
sourced from the equipment vendor or the panel supplier.

**Where the big savings are real:** exterior skin on a mid-rise, replacing
scaffold work — *"six crew instead of sixty or seventy."* **That win does not
transfer to interior partitions or low-rise.**

**And the counter-evidence:** HUD/NAHB's matched-pair steel-vs-wood demonstration
houses found light-gauge steel stick framing took **~30% MORE labour** than wood,
with a **15–20% first-project cost penalty from the learning curve.** Dated and
residential — but it is the only rigorously matched study in the corpus, and its
message is that **the learning curve is a real, measurable, first-project tax.**

> **At 7–23% you cannot sell a panel to a framing sub on price. The premium comes
> out of the GC's general conditions and financing carry. So you sell to the GC,
> or you do not sell.**

## 8 · The version that works

**Panelising for your own framing crews is a different business, and almost every
finding above stops applying.**

| | Third-party sale | **Own crews** |
|---|---|---|
| Merchant margin required | yes, and unknown | **no — internal transfer** |
| Delegated design | likely, per spec | **none — fabricate to the construction documents exactly as your own field crew would** |
| Middleman shield | **forfeited** | **irrelevant — the installing entity was already the responsible party** |
| Utilization risk | a sales pipeline | **solved by your own backlog** |
| Question to answer | can we sell panels? | **does shop assembly beat field assembly for us?** — a 7–23% question on a base you control |

**AISI S202-20 is explicit** that the information required for a panelised
installation *"is the same as is required for a contractor field framing the
structure,"* and that a wall panel placement diagram *"requires no engineering
input… does not require the seal of any wall panel designer or registered design
professional."*

**And Ben is the asset that makes this version work.** A career framer knows
which walls are worth panelising and which are not. That judgement is the whole
business.

### The five steps

**0 · Three phone calls before spending a dollar.** KY DHBC on whether panels are
"industrialized building systems." TN Fire Marshal on the insignia. A KY permit
service on the divisible-load question. **Kill criterion: if Kentucky says
per-model approval applies, stop. Custom work cannot live inside that regime.**

**1 · One project, internal only.** Non-load-bearing interior partitions or
exterior backup. **Screws only — write "no shop or field welding" into the scope**
and you delete AWS D1.3, welder qualification, WPS submittals, weld inspection
and hexavalent chromium controls in one sentence. Seal nothing, offer no
engineering. Existing floor space, one table — a used Triad line runs **$50,000**.
**Capital exposure under $100K.**

**2 · Instrument it exactly like the cut-shop study.** Time-and-motion on shop
hours per sf **and** field hours per sf **and** freight per sf, against a matched
field-framed area on the same job. **Kill criterion: if total man-hours don't beat
field framing by at least 7% after the learning curve, or freight per sf exceeds
the labour saving, stop.**

**3 · Write the AISI S240 Chapter D quality manual anyway.** Named QC inspector,
written QC program, product identification, inspection records, shop drawing
control. Costs weeks, not dollars — and it is the prerequisite for approved-
fabricator status under IBC §1704.2.5.1 and for any future third-party sale.
**Doing it while the customer is captive and the stakes are low is the highest-
leverage move in this plan.**

**4 · Third-party sales only after that, and only to GCs — never to framing
subs.** The sub cannot pay the premium; the GC can. **Sell crew scarcity, not
labour hours** — AGC's 2025 survey has **88% of firms with unfilled craft
openings**, the highest in three years. A GC who cannot staff a six-storey frame
doesn't need a labour-hour comparison; they need the wall to exist. *That is a
scarcity sale and scarcity carries price.*

**5 · The volume gate.** No dedicated building and no fourth shop employee until
the framing operation alone generates **150,000+ sf/yr of panelisable wall with
twelve months of visible backlog.**

---

## 9 · And the thing worth considering instead

Your competitive asset is not a factory. **It is a career framer and a cut shop
on I-65.**

> **Sell the framing labour with the material.** Become the CFS framing
> subcontractor rather than the panel manufacturer, and panelise only where it
> makes your own crews faster.

That captures the labour-scarcity premium, keeps the middleman-to-manufacturer
transition off the table, requires no model-plan approvals, no delegated design,
and no minimum factory utilization.

**It is also exactly what Tri-State and Great Lakes both converged on** — both
sell installation, not just panels. When your two nearest competitors
independently arrive at the same model, that is the market telling you something.
