# Brand Platform — Misty Valley Supply

**Bonnieville, Kentucky · I-65 between Louisville and Nashville**
Founding partners: Ben Easterday, Christopher Easterday, Joey Allee

*This document governs how the company describes itself: on the storefront, on
the phone, on an invoice, and on the day something goes wrong. It is written to
be used, not admired. Where it conflicts with a piece of copy already live, the
copy changes.*

---

## 1 · Positioning statement

For the roofing and framing contractors working the I-65 corridor between
Louisville and Nashville — the small and mid-size subs who buy fall protection,
PPE and rooftop material a job at a time — Misty Valley Supply is the supply
house run by people who have been on the roof. The national catalogs will sell
you a part number and leave the code question on your desk; the counter down the
road will sell you whatever came in on the last truck and tell you it is the
same thing. We do neither. Every item we list is tied to the consensus standard
it is built to and the OSHA paragraph that requires it, so a foreman can search
**1926.501** and get the things that actually satisfy it. What we cannot buy
right, we fabricate in our own shop and stand behind as a formal substitution,
with sealed calculations, not a claim of "or equal." The result is that a
contractor on this corridor can buy at 6 a.m. from a phone, take delivery the
same day, and hand an inspector, an architect or a safety manager the paper that
proves what went on the building — without hiring anyone to figure that out for
him.

## 4 · Core values

*Five. Each one names what it costs us. A value with no price attached is
decoration — anyone can claim it, so it distinguishes nobody.*

### 1. The rule is the product.

**What it means:** every item we sell carries the consensus standard it is built
to and the OSHA paragraph that requires it, written in plain language, searchable
by citation. Not a badge. Not "meets or exceeds." The actual rule, the actual
paragraph, and an honest sentence about what it does and does not cover.

**What this costs us:** the upsell. When the rule says a man needs a warning line
and not a $900 anchor package, we publish that, and we lose the difference. It
also costs us maintenance forever — a citation that goes stale is worse than no
citation at all, so somebody has to own it every year, paid, whether or not it
sells a single hard hat.

### 2. An equal has to be equal.

**What it means:** when a drawing names a product and we build to match it — an
SC3 frame with 7.2 Rib panel, say — that is a formal substitution. It goes to an
engineer for sealed calculations and to the architect for written acceptance
before it goes on a roof. We do the engineering first and ask for the order
after.

**What this costs us:** money before revenue, and speed. We pay for calcs on jobs
we do not win. We are slower than the man who ships it Thursday and lets the
inspector sort it out. And we will walk away from a customer who wants the price
of an equal without the paperwork of one — which is the same as saying we will
turn down orders we could physically fill.

### 3. Everybody gets paid.

**What it means:** vendors on the terms we agreed to, subs on time, freight on
time. When cash is tight, the partners are paid last, not first. Nobody who
worked for us waits through a holiday weekend because cutting a check on Friday
was inconvenient for us.

**What this costs us:** cash, at the worst possible moment. That is the point — a
value that only applies when it is affordable is a preference. This one has
already cost us: it is the reason the company exists in the shape it does.

### 4. We go and look.

**What it means:** somebody from this company stands on the roof we supplied and
in the shop that fabricated it. We do not run this business from a screen, and we
do not learn about a bad load from a phone call.

**What this costs us:** windshield time and selling days. Every hour on a
jobsite is an hour not quoting, and there is no line on the P&L that pays it
back. We do it anyway, and we will keep doing it when we are big enough that it
looks inefficient.

### 5. Nothing lives in one man's head.

**What it means:** if a thing only happens because a specific person did it, it
is not a system — it is a favor, and favors do not survive funerals. Every
process here gets written down and taught to somebody else. At all times there
are at least three people who could run this without a founder, by name.

**What this costs us:** ego, and the real pleasure of being the only one who can
do a thing. It also costs us speed, constantly: writing it down and teaching it
is always slower this week than just doing it. We know exactly what the other
choice costs, because a hundred-year-old company in one of our families ended in
a drawer.

## 5 · Voice and tone

**The sound to aim for:** a person who has been on a roof, telling you something
you need to know, in the fewest words that will do it. Short sentences.
Specifics over adjectives. Numbers where numbers exist. No exclamation points. No
jokes about safety. Never chummy about money.

**The competitive read:** Grainger and Fastenal are competent and forgettable —
they describe. Home Depot Pro shouts. QXO talks like a deck. We talk like the guy
who unloaded it.

---

### Rule 1 — Say the number.

Adjectives are what people use when they do not know the spec. Give dimensions,
weights, ratings and citations, and let the reader draw the conclusion.

**Say this** *(product page)*
> **Non-penetrating steel roof guardrail — 10 ft section.**
> 42 in. top rail, 21 in. mid rail, 165 lb per section with base. Satisfies
> 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10) for low-slope roofs when installed at the roof edge per
> the manufacturer's instructions. Two men to set a section. In stock in
> Bonnieville; pick up today or on the truck tomorrow.

**Not this**
> Premium heavy-duty guardrail system. Engineered for maximum protection and
> peace of mind — the industry's most trusted name in fall protection.

---

### Rule 2 — Bad news goes in the first sentence, with the way out.

Nobody reads to the bottom of a paragraph to find out they are shut off. Lead
with the fact, give the number, name a human being, and say what clears it.

**Say this** *(credit hold notice)*
> **Your account is on hold and nothing ships until it clears.**
> Invoice 1042 is 47 days out. Terms are net 30. You can pay it here, or call
> Christopher at (270) 000-0000 — if there is a reason, we would rather hear it
> than guess at it. We have been on the other side of a late check and we are not
> going to pretend otherwise.

**Not this**
> Due to your current account status, order fulfillment has been temporarily
> suspended pending resolution. Please contact our accounts receivable department
> at your earliest convenience.

---

### Rule 3 — Warn like a foreman, not a lawyer.

A warning exists to stop somebody from doing the wrong thing, not to protect us
in a deposition. Name the product, name the gap, say who does what next, and say
what we will not do.

**Say this** *(substitution warning on a roof screen page)*
> **This is not the product on your drawings.**
> The basis of design is a RoofScreen SC3 frame with 7.2 Rib panel. What we build
> is an equal — our fabrication, engineered to the same loads. That makes it a
> formal substitution: it needs sealed calculations and the architect's written
> acceptance before it goes on the roof. We will furnish the calcs. You have to
> get the acceptance. If the architect says no, we do not ship it, and you owe us
> nothing.

**Not this**
> Comparable to RoofScreen SC3! Same great performance at a fraction of the cost.
> Meets or exceeds all applicable industry standards.

---

### Rule 4 — Show our own cut.

When we are explaining a fee, we show the arithmetic including what we keep.
Anyone who hides the number is about to raise it.

**Say this** *(The Yard — fee explanation)*
> **The Yard costs the buyer nothing. Sellers pay 5%.**
> On a $4,600 skid steer: you get $4,370, we bill $230, the card processor takes
> $133.70 out of that, and we keep $96.30. Your buyer's card is authorized when
> you agree on the deal and charged when he confirms pickup — we never hold his
> money in between, because holding it would make us something we are not
> licensed to be. Below about $7 the processor's flat fee eats the entire
> commission, so do not run a box of screws through it.

**Not this**
> Low, transparent fees! The Yard takes a small commission to help keep the
> lights on and the community thriving.

---

### Rule 5 — Be dry. Never be cute.

Humor is allowed exactly where nothing is at stake, and even there it is dry, not
performing. The reader is on a roof with one bar of signal.

**Say this** *(404)*
> **That page is not here.**
> It was either a product we stopped carrying or a link that was wrong to begin
> with. Search the site, or call the store at (270) 000-0000 and tell somebody
> what you were looking for.

**Not this**
> Oops! Looks like you wandered off the jobsite. 🚧 404 — this page must be on
> break. Let's get you back to safety!

## 6 · Naming and nomenclature

**The company.**
- Legal and first-mention form: **Misty Valley Supply**. No comma, no "Co.", no
  "LLC" outside of contracts, invoices and filings.
- Short form on second reference and in conversation: **Misty Valley**. This is
  the correct short form, and it is deliberate — the name was on the trucks for
  ten years before it was on a storefront, and shortening to "Misty Valley"
  inherits that. **Never "MVS"** in anything a customer sees. `mvs-` is a file
  prefix, not a brand.
- **Never** "Misty Valley Supply Company," "MV Supply," or "misty valley supply"
  set in all lowercase as a styling choice.
- **Misty Valley Contracting** is a separate, dissolved company and is named only
  where the history is being told accurately. Supply does not borrow
  Contracting's reputation in marketing copy; it earns its own.

**The two products.**
- **The Yard** — the definite article is part of the name and is always
  capitalized: *"List it on The Yard."* Never "Misty Valley Yard," never "the
  Yard marketplace," never "MV Yard." Watch the collision with the literal gravel
  yard in Bonnieville: **The Yard** is the marketplace; **the yard** is the place
  the forklift lives. If a sentence is ambiguous, rewrite the sentence.
- **Job Site Earth** — three words, capitalized, no hyphen. The trade writes
  "jobsite" as one word and we do too in every other context, so the split here
  is intentional and must not be corrected to "Jobsite Earth" or "JobSite Earth."
  It is a map of job sites and of the earth; the space is the joke and the joke is
  quiet. Never abbreviate to "JSE."
- Both are products **of** Misty Valley Supply, built **by** Allerion Technologies
  LLC. Written correctly: *"The Yard, from Misty Valley Supply."* Allerion is
  named in software, licensing and government contexts — never as a co-brand on a
  hard hat.

**Citations.**
- First use in a block of copy: **29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10)**. After that,
  **1926.501** alone is fine. Consensus standards get the full designation on
  first use — *ANSI/ASSP Z359.11* — then the short form.
- Search terms are written the way a foreman types them: `1926.501`, not
  "OSHA Fall Protection Standard 1926.501."

**The descriptor under the logo.** One line, no tagline in the lockup:

> **Jobsite safety and building materials · Bonnieville, Kentucky**

Where the lockup is too small for that (embroidery, a truck door, a favicon),
drop to the place alone: **Bonnieville, Kentucky**. Do not shorten by deleting
the town — the town is the credential.

### What we sell

Misty Valley Supply is a supply house in Bonnieville, Kentucky, on I-65 between
Louisville and Nashville. We sell roof safety and edge protection — guardrail,
warning line, anchors, hole covers — and the gear that goes with it: hard hats,
safety glasses, gloves, hi-vis vests, harnesses. We fabricate rooftop equipment
screens in our own shop and sell them as a kit or by the piece. Cold-formed metal
studs are coming, when we can do them right.

### The Easterdays

Ben Easterday grew up Amish and has spent about ten years in construction. He ran
Misty Valley Contracting and dissolved it. He owns a Bobcat skid steer and knows
what to do with it. His brother Christopher is in this with him.

Ben is the one who started this. He called Joey Allee and asked him to come build
the distribution side of it — not to invest, to build it.

### Allee Lumber

Joey's family had a lumber company. Allee Lumber Company, five generations, more
than a hundred years, started in Illinois. "Big Jim" founded it in the early
1900s. It went to his son James Byron — "Little Jim," who played football at
Northwestern. Then to James Roger. Then to Joey's father. It would have come to
Joey.

It is gone.

Joey's account of it, in his own words: *"My failure was I didn't lead when my
family needed a leader."*

We are not going to dress that up or use it to sell anything. A hundred years of
other people's work ended, and the man who would have inherited it says he did
not step forward in time. It stays on this page because it is the reason this
company is built the way it is. Every process here gets written down and taught
to somebody else, because a business that works only as long as one man is
willing to carry it on his back is not a business. It is a favor. Favors end.

### Why the two families are doing this together

Ben has the trade. Ten years of framing and hanging in Kentucky, a shop, a yard,
a truck, and a name that already meant a crew that showed up. Joey has the front
end: he is the chief estimator at R&B Roofing, and reading drawings and
specifications is what he does for a living. He also runs Allerion Technologies,
which builds the software this store runs on.

Neither half is worth much alone. A supply house run by people who have never
been on a roof sells you what is in stock and lets you find out on the building
whether it was right. An estimator with no shop behind him can tell you exactly
what the job needs and then hand you nothing. Put them together and you get a
supplier who reads the set before he quotes, and who has the material on the
ground forty minutes from your job.

That is the whole reason for the partnership. There is no better story than that
one, and we are not going to invent one.

### What we promise

**We will tell you the rule.** Every product on this site cites the consensus
standard it is built to and the OSHA paragraph that requires it. Search 1926.501
and you will get the things that satisfy it. If the honest answer is that you
need less than we would like to sell you, that is still the answer you get.

**An equal will actually be equal.** When your drawings name a product and we
build to match it, that is a formal substitution, and it goes to an engineer for
sealed calculations before it goes on your roof. We pay for that whether or not
we end up with the order. If the architect will not accept it, we do not ship it.

**Everybody gets paid.** Vendors on terms, subs on time, and when money is tight
the partners are paid last. We know how the other way feels because we have stood
in it.

That is the company: a shop, a yard, a truck, three men, and a short list of
things we do not do.

## 8 · Ten taglines, ranked

| # | Tagline | Why it works, and where it is weak |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **We read the spec.** | Four words, one verb, a person speaking. Encodes the actual differentiator (the citation database, the substitution discipline, an estimator as a partner) as a habit rather than a claim, and it survives every product they ever add. |
| 2 | **Ask us what rule it satisfies.** | The most specific line on the list and an invitation rather than a boast — no national catalog can say it without building what we built. Long for a lockup, and it asks the customer to do something before he trusts us. |
| 3 | **Every part cites its rule.** | Literally true, checkable in one click, and it is the whole product strategy in four words. Slightly cold — it describes a database, not a person. |
| 4 | **Built by people who have been on the roof.** | The credibility line, and the reason the tone works at all. Weak as a lockup because half the category claims some version of it; strong as an About-page opener. |
| 5 | **Proof, not "or equal."** | Sharpest line for the substitution story and it draws blood with the right audience. Too inside-baseball for anyone buying gloves. |
| 6 | **Safety you can prove.** | Clean, benefit-first, and points at the paper rather than the fear. Reads a little like a national brand; three other companies could run it tomorrow. |
| 7 | **The hard thing, on time.** | Carries the Roosevelt ethos without quoting it, and doubles as an operating standard. Vague on its own — needs the store around it to mean anything. |
| 8 | **Forty minutes from your job.** | Local, concrete, and the one advantage Grainger cannot buy. Ties the brand to a radius it will eventually outgrow. |
| 9 | **Bonnieville, Kentucky. On the corridor.** | Place as credential, quiet and confident. Says nothing about what is sold, so it only works next to a product. |
| 10 | **Nothing here is a guess.** | Good spine, good for a values page. As a tagline it is defined by what it is not, which is the weakest way to say anything. |

### Recommendation

> ## We read the spec.

It is the only line on the list that is a differentiator, a proof and a promise
at the same time — a national catalog cannot say it, the counter down the road
will not, and the customer can test it on the first phone call. It also describes
the work rather than the merchandise, so it still fits when the company is
selling studs and Division 8 openings instead of hard hats, and it puts the
brand's voice in the first person, where it belongs.

## 9 · What to avoid

Five clichés this category defaults to. Each is fatal here for a specific reason.

**1. "Your trusted partner in safety."**
Unfalsifiable, and it is the exact register every distributor in America already
occupies. Worse, it competes for space with the one claim we make that a customer
can actually check — the citation on the product page. Trading a checkable fact
for an unprovable feeling is a straight downgrade, and it is the downgrade this
industry makes by reflex.

**2. "Safety first."**
It is printed on the banner at the entrance to every job where the guardrail is
missing. Using it puts us on the side of the poster instead of the side of the
man on the roof, and it is the language of compliance theater — the precise thing
our product is built to replace. Anyone who has been on a roof hears it as noise.

**3. "One-stop shop / everything you need under one roof."**
False, and losable. We do not stock everything, and the moment we invite the
comparison we are being measured against companies with 1,300 branches and 4,000
trucks. It picks the one fight we cannot win and abandons the one we can, which
is knowing what the job actually requires before anybody buys anything.

**4. Heritage pathos — "Five generations. Over a century of family tradition."**
The Allee lumber company is gone. Selling its hundred years as a credential for a
new company is a lie of implication, and it converts the most honest paragraph
we have into marketing. The history earns its place on the About page only
because it is told as a loss and as a reason for how the company is organized.
The instant it becomes a badge, it stops being true and starts being cheap.

**5. Blue-collar cosplay — hard-hat-at-sunrise stock photography, flags, distressed
all-caps slab type, "the hardworking men and women who build America."**
Our entire audience is sold this image daily by companies that have never carried
a bundle up a ladder, and they can smell it from the parking lot. It is also the
opposite of the ethos the company actually runs on: the strenuous life is about
doing the hard thing, not about looking like someone who does. A brand that
performs toughness is telling you it has nothing checkable to offer.
