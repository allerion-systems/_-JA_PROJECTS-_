# Vending, PPE, and the Goggles

Prepared for **Joey Allee**, 5 September 2026.

> The ask was: *"online store first… safety… develop a vending machine
> program"* — and, standing since day one, 3D-printed made-to-order
> construction safety goggles.

Two of these are hard. One of them is a liability trap and I am going to be
direct about it, because you asked me to be.

---

## 1. Vending: one company publishes numbers, and its placements are shrinking

Fastenal is the only participant in this industry with audited, disclosed unit
economics. Everyone else — Grainger KeepStock, CribMaster, AutoCrib, SupplyPro,
Apex, IVM — publishes nothing. **In a healthy category with strong unit
economics, at least one more player would be bragging with numbers.**

**Fastenal FY2025** ([10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/815556/000081555626000009/fast-20251231.htm)):

| | |
|---|---:|
| FASTVend devices in field | ~124,000 |
| Weighted installations (MEU) | **136,638** (+7.6%) |
| **Weighted signings (MEU)** | **25,892 — down 7.5% YoY** |
| FASTBin/FASTVend sales | $2,675.0M (32.2%) |
| Total managed-inventory sales | $3,712.7M (**44.7%** of revenue) |
| Gross margin / SG&A | 45.0% / 24.8% |

**The MEU is the Rosetta Stone.** Fastenal defines one machine-equivalent unit
as **$2,000/month of targeted throughput.** Do the division: $2,675.0M ÷ 136,638
= **$19,577 per MEU per year ≈ $1,631/month.** The installed base runs at
**~82% of its own design target.** Nobody advertises that number.

> 🔴 **The category leader's placement signings fell 7.5% in 2025, and it cut
> its own goal mid-year** from 28,000–30,000 MEU to 25,000–26,000 — in a year
> its total revenue grew 8.7%. **Placement growth is decelerating while revenue
> accelerates.** The machine is customer-acquisition cost, not a product.

### The structural fact everyone gets wrong: you are the bank

Fastenal's balance sheet carries **"Shelving, industrial vending, and equipment
— $1,524.6M gross"**, depreciated over 3–10 years and booked as an *occupancy*
expense. FY2025 capex included $160.1M in that bucket. And the word
"consignment" appears **zero times** in the 10-K — the inventory sitting in the
machine is Fastenal's until an employee badges in and pulls a part.

**You finance the box, the software, the cellular connection, and the stock
inside it, and you get paid in trickles.** That is the same working-capital wall
we have hit in every model in this engagement, with a $13,000 steel cabinet
bolted to the front of it.

Real published prices (The Safety Source, live list): helix PPE/MRO vender
**$13,000** outright, smart locker **$8,000**, or **$1,000/month on a 36-month
agreement** — $36,000 against a $13,000 purchase. Software plus cellular
**$1,200/year**. **The "free machine" is never free; it is amortized into
product price.**

### The threshold, derived rather than asserted

Nobody publishes it, so here is the arithmetic from Fastenal's own margins
(45.0% GM − 24.8% SG&A = 20.2% operating):

| Throughput | Operating profit/yr | Payback on a $10–13K machine |
|---|---:|---|
| $2,000/mo ($24,000/yr) | ~$4,850 | **2–3 years** — before funding the inventory |
| $1,000/mo ($12,000/yr) | ~$2,425 | 4–6 years — **marginal to negative** |
| **To recover in 12 months** | — | **~$49,500/yr ≈ $4,125/mo — double the flagship target** |

Hold that last number. It is the entire job-site verdict.

*(Do not confuse this with Fastenal's disclosed **site** threshold — *"customer
sites with monthly sales potential of $50,000 or more"* — that is the bar for a
dedicated Onsite presence, not for a machine. A lot of secondary commentary
conflates them.)*

### The savings claims do not survive contact

Published figures — 15–20%, 20%, 25–30% on PPE, 34%, "up to 30%" — are **all
published by parties selling vending machines.** The one peer-reviewed study
(Williams et al., *IJOPM* 2016) is a **survey of *perceived* implementation
success using structural equation modeling**, not a measurement of consumption.
Its actual finding is that vending works when the partner relationship works.

And the mechanism is confounded four ways: real accountability savings, shrink
that is really theft accounting, **SKU rationalization done during
implementation that would have saved money anyway**, and price effects from
consolidating spend. Fastenal's own 10-K concedes the direction: larger managed
customers *"typically generate higher volume at lower gross margins."*

> **Honest read: badge-level accountability is real and probably worth 10–20% on
> PPE. Anything above 20% is vendor arithmetic. A plan built on 30–45% is
> building on sand.**

## 2. The job-site gap: tried by the incumbent, for ten years, quietly

This was the version I thought was genuinely underserved. It is not — it is
**partially tried and quietly underperforming**, and the failure is structural.

**Fastenal built exactly this product.** FAST P.O.D. — a blue shipping container
fitted as an unmanned mini-store with vending inside, **security cameras, locks,
HVAC, and an intercom to a help desk for after-hours access** — launched around
2016 and explicitly marketed for *"job sites."* The
[FASTPOD product page](https://www.fastenal.com/fast/services-and-solutions/jobsite-solutions)
is live today.

> 🔴 **The strings "POD", "FASTPOD", "jobsite" and "job site" appear ZERO times
> in Fastenal's entire FY2025 Form 10-K.**

Fastenal itemizes 21 vending device models, four FASTBin sensor technologies,
its truck fleet count, and its NASCAR sponsorship — but after a decade does not
consider the job-site program worth a sentence to shareholders. It was absent
from the Q2 2026 call too.

### The end-market data says construction resists this model

**Fastenal, non-residential construction as a share of sales:** 9.1% (2023) →
8.5% (2024) → **8.1% (2025)**. Falling for three straight years. Manufacturing
is 75.9% and rising — that is where the flywheel spins.

**And the single best datapoint in this whole report, from Grainger FY2025:**

| Contractors as % of revenue | |
|---|---:|
| High-Touch Solutions N.A. (branches, KeepStock, onsite service) | **6%** |
| Endless Assortment (Zoro — pure e-commerce, zero service) | **12%** |

**Contractors are twice as concentrated in the no-service channel as in the
managed-inventory channel.** Construction buyers self-select *away* from managed
inventory and *toward* transactional purchasing. That is revealed preference
across billions of dollars, and it cuts straight through the thesis.

### What does exist tells you what the business actually is

- **United Rentals** delivers on-site tool cribs and **managed 53-ft trailers —
  staffed by a United Rentals employee** who scans tools in and out. The labor
  is the product; the crib is packaging.
- **Hilti** — the most sophisticated construction-direct operator there is —
  solved the same problem **without hardware**: ON!Track asset software +
  Automatic Refill + **Consumables Pass, a flat monthly subscription.**
- **Trailer- or skid-mounted industrial vending does not exist as a product
  category.** Mobile solar/battery power is a solved commodity. Nobody has
  bolted a vender to one. That is either the gap or the graveyard.

### Why it fails, ranked

**1. The math — dispositive.** A machine that recovers its cost in 12 months
needs **~$50,000/year of throughput, double the flagship target.** A 6–18 month
project means the asset earns its whole keep at double normal productivity and
*then* survives teardown, transport, refurbishment and idle time. **Industrial
vending assumes multi-year amortization against a permanent crib. Remove
permanence and the model doesn't bend — it inverts.**

**2. Who signs and who pays — the real reason nobody has cracked it.**
Consumables are bought by **each subcontractor**, not the GC. Dozens of separate
legal entities per project, each with its own supply agreements and its own
reasons not to let another trade pull from its stock. **The GC has the site, the
power and the security but not the spend. The subs have the spend but not the
site or the duration.** In a plant, one entity owns both — that is precisely why
it works there. This is a structural mismatch, not an execution problem.

**3. Attribution.** The savings mechanism *is* badge-level accountability. Job
sites have no badge system spanning employers, and workers turn over between
trades and phases. Without attribution the machine is an expensive locked
cabinet — and you still have to decide whose account gets billed when the
electrician's apprentice pulls the drywall crew's blades.

**4. Theft.** Indicative aggregator figures put US construction equipment theft
at $300M–$1B/year with recovery under 25%. Note the irony: **the product's core
value proposition is shrink reduction, deployed into the one environment where
shrink risk against the machine itself is highest.**

**5. Power and connectivity are solved and cheap** — so anyone pitching that as
the hard part hasn't found the hard part.

> **If you want to keep this alive, follow the evidence: sell the GC a *service*
> — staffed or subscription replenishment across all subs on the site, priced
> per project — and treat hardware as an optional fixture. Hilti and United
> Rentals both figured that out already, which is itself the warning.**

**Cheapest test:** call three commercial GCs and ask who bought the blades and
gloves on their last project. **If the answer is "each sub," this is over.**

## 3. The goggles — do not build this

I read the full text of **ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2020**. Everything below is from the
standard itself, not from a blog. (Note: **Z87.1-2025 superseded it, published
8 January 2026.** Buy your own copy from ANSI.)

### What the test actually is

**High Mass Impact (§7.1.4.2):** a **pointed steel projectile, ≥500 g, 30°
conical tip, dropped from ≥127 cm (50 in.)** — about **6.2 joules concentrated
on a 3.18 mm tip.**
**High Velocity Impact (§7.1.4.3):** a **6.35 mm steel ball at 45.7 m/s
(150 ft/s)** for spectacles.

**And here is the sentence that kills the idea (§7.1.4.1) — the device fails if:**

> *"any part, fragment or material visible to the unaided eye becomes detached
> from the inner surface of any complete device… **fracture**… penetration of
> the inner surface either by the projectile passing completely through the
> lens, **frame or housing component**… lens not retained."*

**"Fracture" of the complete device is a failure. The frame is in scope.**

**Sample size:** **ten complete devices** per lens retention system — six high
velocity, four high mass. **"Failure of any one device… shall constitute failure
for a set."**

Plus optical tolerances, ≥85% luminous transmittance, minimum coverage, lateral
coverage with no opening >1.5 mm, and an **ignition test (§9.7)** — a steel rod
at **650 °C pressed against every externally exposed surface for 5 seconds**
without igniting or continuing to glow.

### Three corrections to what you'll read online

**1. Z87.1 is self-certified. There is no third-party mandate.** §2.3.2:
*"**Manufacturers**… shall ensure that all required tests have been performed."*
The words "third party," "independent," and "accredit" do not appear in a
product-certification sense anywhere in the standard. Safety blogs asserting
"rigorous third-party testing is required" are **wrong as a matter of text.**

**But self-certification is a trap, not a gift.** Buyers demand a Declaration of
Conformity or a lab report. And in litigation, *"we self-certified"* without a
lab report is not a defense — **it is the plaintiff's opening statement.**

**2. OSHA regulates the employer, not you.** 29 CFR 1926.102(b)(1) requires
eyewear complying with **Z87.1-2010, -2003, or -1989** — OSHA does not
incorporate the 2020 or 2025 editions at all. OSHA cannot cite you for making
bad glasses; it cites the GC whose worker was wearing them, **and then that GC
sues you.**

**3. The "printed frame + certified drop-in lens" plan is explicitly
foreclosed.** Four sentences:

- **§7.1.2:** *"Frames and shells shall meet the requirements for high mass
  impact and high velocity impact… **These components shall be tested as a
  complete device.**"*
- **§2.3.2:** *"**All components** of eye and face protectors shall comply."*
- **§2.3.3:** *"**Compliance cannot always be assured when components are
  replaced.**"*
- **§2.3.4:** *"**Non-compliant components shall not be used with… compliant
  components.**"*

**There is no path where a certified lens confers compliance on an uncertified
frame.** Dropping certified polycarbonate into a printed frame produces, by the
standard's own definition, a non-compliant protector — and marking it `Z87+`
would be a false marking on a safety-critical product.

### Can printed polymer pass? Almost certainly not — and nobody has

FDM parts are anisotropic; layer boundaries are weaker than the extruded
polymer. **A frame printed in layers has a crack-initiation plane built in at
every layer line, and the test's failure criterion is precisely crack
propagation.** Published comparisons find notched Izod impact strength lower for
printed parts than for injection-molded parts of the same composition.

- **FDM PLA/PETG** — brittle, Tg ~60 °C. Not a candidate.
- **SLA/DLP resin** — the worst option. Photopolymer thermosets shatter into
  fragments, and §7.1.4.1 fails a device on *any detached fragment*. **An SLA
  frame is a fragment generator pointed at an eye.**
- **SLS PA12 nylon** — the only serious candidate. Tough, near-isotropic, already
  used in commercial eyewear (Materialise/Safilo, YOU MAWO). But no source
  claims any SLS frame is Z87.1 impact-rated, and SLS parts are porous and
  moisture-absorbing, which matters for the ignition test.
- **FDM polycarbonate** — does not reproduce injection-molded PC performance;
  severe warping on thin sections.

**No 3D-printed product of any kind appears to carry a documented Z87.1
certification** — not frames, not goggles, not faceshields. The closest analogue
is the COVID face-shield episode, where NIH/FDA/VA stated they *"cannot ensure
the quality, safety, and efficacy of these designs when manufactured without
proper quality controls"* — and those were never presented as Z87.1-compliant.

*(Luxexcel's 3D-printed lenses met the **FDA drop-ball test** — 5/8" ball,
0.56 oz, 50 in, roughly **0.2 J**. That is **more than an order of magnitude
below** Z87 high velocity. It is evidence they passed a much easier test.)*

### And "made to order" is incompatible with certification, by definition

**§7.1.4.6:** type testing *"**shall be performed when any substantive change in
production occurs**"* that could affect the ability to pass.
**§7.1.4.5:** lens qualification extends only to lenses *"made by the **same
manufacturer, from the same material, with the same coatings and processes**."*

**Every custom geometry is a new configuration.** Resize a frame to a scanned
face and you change rim cross-section, bridge thickness, hinge geometry, temple
length and lens-retention bevel — every one of them a load path in a 6.2 J point
impact. **You would be testing and destroying ten devices per customer.**

The only coherent version is a **small fixed family of certified sizes,
type-tested once each, with zero per-customer variation** — which is
conventional eyewear manufacturing, competing against injection molders selling
Z87+ glasses at **$3–8 wholesale.**

### The liability asymmetry

PPE is a **failure-to-protect** product — the most exposed category there is.
The damage is catastrophic and permanent, the causal chain is short and vivid,
and the plaintiff's case is one sentence: *here is the standard, here is the
test, your product never passed it.*

**Additive manufacturing makes the defense worse, not better.** Injection molding
gives you process capability data and lot traceability. Printing gives you a
build file, a machine that drifted, and per-unit variability you cannot
characterize — **meaning you cannot prove the specific unit that failed matched
the unit that was tested.** That is a manufacturing-defect claim you cannot
rebut. And made-to-order removes the type-test defense entirely, because no type
test covers that unit.

**Assessment, labeled as assessment:** a startup selling printed eyewear as PPE
without third-party test reports is likely to be **declined by standard
insurance markets**, and any surplus-lines quote will carry a PPE or
failure-to-perform exclusion.

### And there is no unregulated version

Abandon "safety" and sell them as fashion, and **21 CFR 801.410** still applies
to *all* eyeglasses and sunglasses sold in the US: lenses must be impact
resistant, **tested on "a statistically significant sampling of lenses from each
production batch,"** with **invoices, shipping documents, sales records and test
results kept for 3 years** and available to FDA on request.

## 4. Verdict, and the two cheap tests

**3D-printed, made-to-order safety eyewear is a liability trap, not a product.**
Five independent reasons, any one sufficient:

1. The standard requires the **complete device, frame included**, to survive a
   500 g pointed steel cone from 50 inches **without fracture**. Layer-line
   anisotropy is a crack-initiation plane engineered directly into the failure
   mode being tested.
2. The drop-in-certified-lens workaround is **explicitly foreclosed**.
3. **"Made to order" is incompatible with type certification.**
4. **Zero precedent anywhere.** When a category is that attractive and that
   unoccupied, assume it has been attempted and abandoned, not overlooked.
5. **The asymmetry is absurd** — a few dollars of margin on a commodity that
   sells for $3–8 wholesale, against a blinded worker and an undefendable claim.

### What is not stupid

- **Non-PPE eyewear** — fashion, readers, sports. Still 21 CFR 801.410 batch
  testing and 3-year records. A real business; a completely different one.
- **A certified accessory around a certified device** — printed side-shields,
  prescription carriers worn *behind* a compliant plano protector, fit adapters.
  §2.3.4 still applies, so it still needs testing and careful marking.
- **Fixed-size printed frames, type-tested, no per-customer geometry.** A
  manufacturing-technology bet, not a customization business — and unproven.

### 🔴 The three things to do this month, in order

1. **Send 10 printed prototype frames with certified 2.0 mm polycarbonate plano
   lenses to ICS Laboratories or Intertek for the §9.11 high-mass and §9.12
   high-velocity tests only.** A few thousand dollars, a few weeks. **If they
   fracture — and I expect they will — the entire question is closed for the
   price of a laptop**, before a dollar goes into design, tooling, branding or
   insurance.
2. **Ask a specialty broker to quote PPE product liability for an additively
   manufactured Z87 product. A decline is a free answer,** and it arrives in a
   week.
3. **Before any job-site vending work, call three commercial GCs and ask who
   bought the blades and gloves on their last project.** If the answer is "each
   sub," §2 is over.

**All three are cheap, fast, and falsifying. That is the point.** Spend the few
thousand dollars to be told no, rather than the few hundred thousand to find
out.
