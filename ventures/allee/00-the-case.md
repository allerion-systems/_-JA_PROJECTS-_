## 1. First, the record, because it matters

There are **two records** here and they do not say the same thing. Both are below,
labelled, because the difference matters the moment this goes on a website or in
front of a bank.

### The newspaper record

**Allee Wholesale Lumber Company, organised 1940.** James A. ("Big Jim") and
James Byron ("Little Jim") Allee. Main office moved to Mattoon, Illinois in July
1951, with a branch in Tupelo, Mississippi. Sixteen states. Hardwoods exported to
Ireland. Lumber for the Willow Run housing project and Scott Field Air Base
during the war. Thirty-five to forty million board feet a year — **most of which
they never saw.**

### The family record — and what the archives say about it

The family account is that the business **did not start in 1940 and did not start
in Illinois.** It began as **a lumber yard in Tupelo, Mississippi**, making it
**over a hundred years across five generations**; the 1940 date is the
organisation of the *wholesale trading arm*. And that **James Byron Allee built
the business on the network he made playing football at Northwestern and then as
kicker for the 1928 and 1929 Chicago Cardinals**, where he was also a backup
tailback.

**Verification run 5 September 2026. The results are below and they are not
comfortable.**

#### 🔴 The Chicago Cardinals claim is contradicted by the surviving record

Full rosters **and complete scoring tables survive for both seasons**, and there
is no Allee on either.
([1928](https://www.profootballarchives.com/1928nflchic.html) ·
[1929](https://www.profootballarchives.com/1929nflchic.html))

- **1928: the Cardinals scored seven points all season and attempted zero field
  goals.** The only placekick converted all year was Hugh Grant's single extra
  point. **There was no kicking role to hold.**
- **1929: the kicking was Ernie Nevers** — 10 extra points, 1 field goal, 85 of
  the team's 154 points — plus one field goal from Chief Elkins. Nevers was also
  *the* tailback, and the men backing him up are named.
- All forty Chicago seasons (1920–1959) were searched, including the 1944
  Card-Pitt merger, across every plausible spelling. Zero hits. The consolidated
  NFL player index for A (1920–2025) runs Alleman → Allen.

**Northwestern is strongly but not conclusively negative** — the 1928 *Syllabus*
contains the varsity football section and has no Allee; the 1925–27 and 1929–30
volumes are not digitised.

> **What is genuinely still open, and it matters:** 1920s professional football
> was not only the NFL. The Cardinals' own 1928 schedule included the Hammond

> is not. **Over three generations a story drifts toward the famous name.** That

#### ⚠️ The Tupelo origin and the hundred-year claim are unverified, with contrary indicators

- The Mississippi Department of Archives and History's National Register survey
  of Tupelo — built on Sanborn maps from 1889, 1899, 1903 and 1929 — **names the
  town's lumber firms and no Allee is among them.** It also records that Tupelo's
  lumber industry was finished as a significant local force **by 1907.**
- The Mattoon History Center's lumber-yard panel lists thirteen yards from 1875
  to 1947 — **no Allee.** *(This one actually supports the newspaper: a wholesale
  house arriving in 1951 would not belong on a retail-yard list.)*
- **⚠️ Worth checking before anything else:** the documented century-old Tupelo
  family lumber business is **Leake and Goodlett, 1904–2019 — 115 years.** It is
  worth confirming the family story has not attached itself to the wrong company.
- The "five generations" claim and "Jeff Allee, the **fourth**-generation owner"
  cannot both be right. **Settle that inside the family first** — it is one phone

## 2. The thing that needs saying out loud

Joey did not lose the family business.

He was not the owner. He did not make the decision. He was not asked. A
fourth-generation owner closed a company in 2023 for reasons that were his to
weigh — and a grandson who was never brought inside the business had no lever to
pull and no standing to pull it. **You cannot be at fault for the outcome of a
decision you were structurally excluded from.**

And on the story that Jim Allee didn't want his grandchildren in the business:
Joey never heard him say it. What Joey heard, and what the newspaper record
supports, is a man who wanted his people to go and grow and find out who they
were. **Those two things are not the same sentence, and only one of them was
ever said in his hearing.**

Whatever was true, it is settled now. The relevant question is not who kept
whom out of what. It is: **what does someone do with generations of
accumulated knowledge about how to move building materials, when the company
that held it is gone and the person who carries it is thirty-something and
already in the trade?**

That is a live question. It has a good answer. Everything below is the answer.

## 5. 🔴 The stress test

### 5.1 You are proposing to relaunch a trading company as a manufacturer

Read the clipping again. **"Most of which is never seen by them."** No yard. No
forklift. No warehouse. Two brothers, a secretary, and a teletype, moving forty
million board feet.

**A sawmill and kiln is the exact opposite of that business.** It is the most
capital-intensive, most fixed-cost, most volume-dependent structure in the whole
forest products chain. Logs in a yard, a headrig, a kiln burning energy for
weeks, and a break-even that does not care how you feel about it.

You would be putting your grandfather's name on a business he deliberately
never ran. That is allowed — but do it knowingly, and do not tell yourself you
are continuing the company. **You would be founding a different one with the
same name.**

### 5.2 Every finding in this engagement has pointed the other way

- `10-the-channel-problem.md` — asset-light intermediaries earned **21–27% ROIC
  in FY2025**; asset-heavy distributors earned **3.6–7.5%**.
- Working capital intensity: **8.1% of revenue** asset-light vs **27.0%**
  stocking.
- `where-the-margin-actually-is.md` — the enterable margin is in **reading and
  coordination**, not in holding inventory.
- `modular-and-the-7-brew-model.md` — **every modular failure was a factory
  built ahead of a repeat-order book.** Katerra, Veev, Skender, Diamond Age,
  Factory OS. A sawmill is a factory.

**A greenfield sawmill in Rosiclare is a factory built ahead of an order book,
in a commodity with a brutal demand picture, in one of the most isolated
counties in Illinois.** If you had brought me this idea cold, I would tell you
it is the single riskiest thing discussed in this entire engagement.

### 5.3 What I do not yet know, and am finding out

Research is running now on: the Illinois SOS record for the Allee entities and
whether the **name is available or reinstatable**; Rosiclare and Hardin County —
river terminal access, road and rail, labor, three-phase power, industrial
sites, and Illinois enterprise/River Edge zone incentives; **greenfield hardwood
mill and kiln capex and the minimum viable MBF/year**; log cost, overrun, and
2026 NHLA-grade lumber prices; the state of US hardwood since the China export
collapse and how many mills have closed; how many mills already compete for logs
within 100 miles of Hardin County; and whether hardwood actually moves by barge
on the Ohio today and what a private dock costs to permit.

**RESOLVED — see `01-rosiclare-verdict.md`.** It came back and it does not
support the plan. Peer-reviewed modelling (Virginia Tech / USDA Forest Service)
puts hardwood sawmills at **exactly zero profit** at 4, 8 and 12 MMBF; at a
return to the **all-time record** hardwood price of June 2022, a 12 MMBF mill
earns ~$856K against ~$2.9M of debt service on a $20M greenfield build. Eastern
production in 2026 is projected to be the **lowest since records began in 1960**.
**35 mills closed in 2025.** Rosiclare has ~1,270 employed people in the whole
county, no rail, no four-lane, no commercial river terminal, and half its log
procurement radius across an unbridged river.

**The name, however, is recoverable for about $250 and the window is closing
right now.** That is the one action this week.

### 5.4 The version that is not crazy

If the numbers come back bad — and I expect at least some of them will — the
adjacent business is right there and it is the one your family actually ran:

> **Be the hardwood merchant, not the sawmill.** Concentration yard, kiln, and
> grading — buying green lumber from the dozens of small mills that already
> exist in southern Illinois, Indiana and Kentucky, drying it, grading it, and
> selling it. **Someone else owns the headrig and the log yard risk. You own the
> grade, the moisture content, and the customer.**

That is the Allee position — **buy the mill's reputation and sell on your own** —
updated by one step of vertical integration rather than five. It needs a
fraction of the capital, it does not require a log supply you have to compete
for, and it is the business the newspaper actually describes.

**And it earns the name honestly.**

## 6. What makes this credible rather than sentimental

The story is not the asset. Plenty of people have a family business in the past
tense.

**The asset is that Joey is an estimator who can read a set of plans, price a
package, and talk to a GC — and who happens to have generations of
institutional memory about how this trade actually works.** The second thing is
worthless without the first. The first is already true.

So the order is:

1. **Make Misty Valley Supply work.** Not as a stepping stone — as the thing.
   A partner who treats Ben's business as a stepping stone deserves to lose it.
2. **Build the Kentucky network** nobody in the family ever built. That is the
   genuinely new contribution and it is the honest claim.
3. **Then, and only on numbers, take the name back out.**

**Grandpa's edge was information arriving faster than anyone else's. That edge
is gone. Joey's edge is knowing what the job needs before the buyout happens.
Same position in the transaction, different century.**

Get in front of it. Don't get in the middle of it.
