# The Constitution of Misty Valley Supply

**Bonnieville, Kentucky**
Adopted ____________ · Founding partners **Ben Easterday**, **Christopher Easterday**, **Joey Allee**

---

## Preamble

In April of 1899 Theodore Roosevelt stood in front of a room in Chicago and said
he had come to preach *"not the doctrine of ignoble ease, but the doctrine of the
strenuous life."* He meant that the life worth having is the one that costs
something, and that the men and the nations who refuse to pay are not spared the
reckoning — they only arrive at it having done nothing.

We did not need the speech to know it. We learned it the week this company was
founded.

**On Monday, 31 August 2026, we closed out Lee Street. On Friday, 4 September, we
finished Texas Roadhouse** — everything but one piece of coping, two DCSM vents,
and seven squares of interior canopy. That job was walking toward liquidated
damages. Ben sent his men. Elias sent six more so R&B would not be put on notice.
The chief estimator spent the week in the field with a bag in his hand instead of
a takeoff on his screen. On Thursday night we broke for two hours, went back at
eight, and worked until midnight. Nobody ate.

**And on Friday the check did not go out, and the holiday pushed it to Tuesday.**

That is the founding fact of this company, and both halves of it matter. The
first half is what we are made of. The second half is what we exist to be better
than.

## Article I — Why We Are Misty Valley

We did not pick this name. **It was already on the trucks.**

For ten years "Misty Valley" has meant a crew that shows up, hangs it right, and
takes the call. That reputation was not bought, advertised, or claimed. It was
accumulated one job at a time by men who framed walls in Kentucky, and it is
worth more than any asset on the balance sheet of the company we are founding.

> **[BEN — the origin of the name goes here, in your words. Do not let anyone
> write it for you, and do not let it be invented. If the true story is that it
> sounded good on a Tuesday, then that is the story, and it is a better one than
> anything a marketing person would put in its place.]**

We take the name because we intend to be worthy of it, and because a supplier
whose name is already good for something starts the race a lap ahead of one that
has to explain itself.

**Supply does not get to spend Contracting's reputation. It has to earn its
own — and it can lose Contracting's while it tries.** That is the first thing
every person who works here is told.

## Article II — The Clock, Not the Time

Jim Collins and Jerry Porras drew a distinction in *Built to Last* that this
company is organized around.

**Telling the time** is standing up and being brilliant. Having the answer,
knowing the price, making the call, carrying the thing on your back. It is
impressive and it works — right up until the person doing it stops.

**Building the clock** is constructing something that keeps telling the time
after you are gone. It is slower, less flattering, and almost nobody does it,
because nobody claps for the clock.

We know exactly what happens to the other kind. **The Allee Wholesale Lumber
Company was organised in 1940 in Mattoon, Illinois. Two brothers and a secretary
moved thirty-five to forty million board feet a year across sixteen states, most
of which they never saw. It lasted eighty-three years and four generations. It
was dissolved in 2023.**

It was an extraordinary machine for telling the time. It was not a clock. When
the last man who knew how to run it stopped running it, it stopped — and
everything those four generations knew went into a drawer.

> **We are not building a company that depends on Ben knowing how to frame, on
> Christopher knowing how to run it, or on Joey knowing how to read a set of
> plans. We are building a company that will still work when none of us are
> here.**

That is the standard every decision in this document is measured against. Not
*"is this smart?"* — **"does this still work without me?"**

**The practical test, applied forever:** if a thing only happens because a
specific person did it, it is not a system, it is a favor. Write it down, teach
it to somebody, and make the company able to do it. **Favors do not survive
funerals.**

## Article III — Purpose

*Why we exist. Never fully achieved. Not a goal — a direction.*

> ### To make the crew's day easier than it was yesterday.

Everything else is method. We happen to do it by supplying cold-formed steel
framing packages, but the steel is not the point. The point is the four hours a
man does not spend cutting studs on a slab, the trip up the lift he does not
have to take twice, and the Friday he goes home on time because the bundle he
needed was on the deck, labeled, on the right floor, in the right order.

**We are in the business of giving people their day back.** The invoice says
steel.

---

## Article IV — Mission

*What we are doing now, and for the next several years. Concrete. Replaceable
when it is done.*

> ### To become the cold-formed steel framing package supplier of record for the I-65 corridor between Louisville and Nashville — the one who shows up with the job already taken off.

In plain terms, for the next five years we:

1. **Take off the job before the buyout.** The supplier who does the takeoff owns
   the package before the bid is let.
2. **Cut, label, bundle and sequence it** by floor and by phase.
3. **Put it on the deck when the crew needs it**, not on a lot when the truck
   was free.
4. **Document it to the lot** so that ten years from now anyone can prove what
   went into that wall.
5. **Get paid, and pay everybody else, on time.**

---

## Article V — The Vision

*One goal. Dated. Big enough to be uncomfortable to say out loud.*

> ## By 31 December 2036:
> ### No commercial steel framing package between Louisville and Nashville gets bought without somebody asking us to price it first.

Not that we win them all. That we are **the call you have to make.** The
supplier a GC's estimator would be embarrassed to admit he did not check.

**The scoreboard beneath it, reviewed every January:**

| | We are winning if |
|---|---|
| **Takeoffs furnished** | more than anyone else in the corridor |
| **On-time to the deck** | 98%+, measured, published internally, no excuses |
| **Repeat rate** | a customer's second order is the metric that matters, not the first |
| **Days to pay a sub** | shorter every year, floor of same-week |
| **People who could run this without a founder** | at least three, by name, at all times |

That last line is the clock. **If that number is zero, nothing above it counts.**

## Article VI — Core Values

*Six. Each one states what it costs. A value with no price attached is
decoration — anybody can claim it, so it distinguishes nobody. These will cost us
orders and margin, and we will pay.*

---

### 1. We sell the labor we save, not the steel we move.

Anybody with a phone can buy steel. Price is not a position; it is a gap in
somebody else's schedule that closes the moment a competitor picks up the same
phone.

**What it means:** every job leaves here cut, labeled, bundled and sequenced, or
it does not leave here as our product.

**What it costs:** we will lose bids to people who throw studs on a flatbed
cheaper than we can. **We let them.** A customer who only wants tonnage is not
our customer, and chasing him teaches us the wrong business.

---

### 2. What we said is in the bundle is in the bundle.

A fire-rated wall is not a wall built from good parts. It is a specific tested
assembly, and the rating belongs to the assembly. **Thinner never preserves a
rating.** Base metal thickness must be at or above 95% of design thickness,
measured as delivered with the coating stripped, and a mill certificate does not
settle it — more than half of the samples that fail certification came from
certified prime coil.

**What it means:** we own a micrometer, we use it on incoming material, we keep
the readings, and **we can produce them ten years later.**

**What it costs:** we will reject loads we already paid for. We will tell a
customer no when the answer he wants is yes. We will occasionally be the only
supplier in the room raising a hand.

> **This one is not negotiable at any price, in any market, for any customer,
> ever.** Everything else in this document can be amended. This cannot. A man's
> life is behind a rated wall and he does not get to inspect it.

---

### 3. The date is the product.

Material is available. Certainty is not. The whole industry's real failure is not
that things cannot be gotten — it is that nobody told the superintendent the
truth about when.

**What it means:** we quote dates we have checked, we tell people the bad number
early instead of the good number late, and **a slipped date gets a phone call
before it gets an excuse.**

**What it costs:** we will lose work by being the only quote with an honest lead
time on it. We will be undercut by people who promise Tuesday and deliver the
following Thursday. **We will still be here when that customer is calling us
back.**

---

### 4. Everybody gets paid.

**This company was founded on a Friday when good men did not get paid for a week
they should not have had to work.** We know exactly how that feels because we
were standing in it.

**What it means:** subs and vendors get paid on the terms we agreed to. When
money is tight, **the partners are the last ones paid, not the first.** If we
cannot make payroll and pay a sub in the same week, the partners eat it. Nobody
who worked for us waits on a holiday weekend because it was inconvenient for us
to cut a check on Friday.

**What it costs:** cash. Sometimes a lot of it, at the worst possible time. That
is the point — a value that only applies when it is affordable is not a value.

---

### 5. We go and look.

The Allee brothers went to the mills four times a year to watch the lumber being
made, in a business where they never saw the goods. **In a business built on
trusting the column, going and looking is the entire quality system.**

**What it means:** somebody from this company physically stands in the supplier's
plant. Somebody from this company walks the job we supplied. **We do not manage
this business from a screen.**

**What it costs:** windshield time, travel money, and days that could have been
spent selling.

---

### 6. Nobody's name is bigger than the company.

Ben's trade, Christopher's operation and Joey's takeoff are what make this work
today. **If any one of them is still load-bearing in ten years, we failed.**

**What it means:** every one of us is under a standing obligation to make himself
replaceable — to write it down, teach it, and hand it off. We promote from
inside. We do not build a hero.

**What it costs:** ego, and the very real pleasure of being the only one who can
do a thing.

---

## Article VII — Preserve the Core, Stimulate Progress

The most expensive mistake a company makes is confusing **what it is** with **how
it currently does it** — defending a method to the death because it feels like
defending the mission.

We separate them here, in writing, on the record, so that nobody ten years from
now has to guess.

| **THE CORE — never changes** | **THE METHOD — must be free to change** |
|---|---|
| The purpose in Article III | Where the steel comes from — China, domestic mill, service center, our own line |
| All six values in Article VI | Whether we import at all |
| The 95% rule and everything behind it | Whether we hold a distributor appointment or trade direct |
| Everybody gets paid | Whether we stock inventory or ship direct from the mill |
| The clock, not the time | Whether we ever roll-form our own studs |
| | Whether we stay in steel alone |
| | What we cut, where we cut it, how many trucks we run |

**Read that right-hand column again.** Every one of those is currently a live
argument, and **none of them is who we are.** The moment "we are an importer" or
"we are a cut shop" becomes an identity instead of a tactic, we have started
defending a method against the facts — and the facts always win eventually.

> **We are a value-added framing package supplier. Sourcing is a decision we make
> every year, not a flag we plant.**

## Article VIII — The Genius of the AND

We refuse the choice between the two businesses in front of us.

```
   THE BOOK                              THE SHOP
   sell it before it lands               cut, label, sequence
   thin margin                           real margin
   almost no capital per load            capital-hungry per load
   scales without funding                scales only with funding
   NO differentiation                    THE differentiation
        │                                     ▲
        └──── builds the volume, the ─────────┘
              credit and the customers
              that pay for the shop
```

**The book is how we get big. The shop is why they keep buying from us.** One
creates volume, credit standing and mill relationships with almost no capital;
the other converts a slice of that volume into margin nobody can match on price.

Anyone who tells us to pick one has not understood either.

The same refusal applies everywhere else it comes up: **fast AND right. Cheap to
serve AND worth paying for. Ambitious AND conservative with other people's
money.** When somebody presents this company with an either/or, the first
question is always whether it is real.

## Article IX — The Lines We Do Not Cross

Short, absolute, and written down so that no one has to be brave alone.

1. **We do not sell material we cannot document.** No load without a packing
   list, a weight ticket and a mill cert. No cert taken on faith without a
   measurement.
2. **We do not certify what we have not tested, and we do not mark what we have
   not earned.** Not a rating, not a listing, not a stamp.
3. **We do not sell into a spec we know the product does not meet**, whatever the
   customer says he is willing to accept. He is not the one who has to live in
   the building.
4. **Joey does not bring his employer's information through this door.** Not bid
   numbers, not the pipeline, not sub pricing. **His skill is his. His employer's
   information never is.** This holds until the day he no longer works there, and
   the day after that.
5. **Every sale between Supply and Contracting is disclosed and priced at arm's
   length, in writing, before it happens.** Related-party dealing is legal.
   Undisclosed related-party dealing is how good companies die.
6. **We do not put a partner's household at risk without that household knowing
   the number.** Every personal guarantee is discussed with the family that
   signs it.
7. **We do not say "the check is coming" when we have not cut it.**

## Article X — Governance

**Three partners. Ben, Christopher, Joey.**

- **Ben** — the trade. Product, crews, customers, and the final word on anything
  that touches a wall.
- **Christopher** — the operation. Cash, yard, trucks, and the final word on
  anything that touches a dollar leaving the building.
- **Joey** — the front end. Takeoff, estimating, packages, and the final word on
  what we tell a customer a job needs.

**Article VI, value 2 — the 95% rule — can be invoked by any one of the three,
alone, and stops the load.** No vote. No override. A partner who invokes it and
turns out to be wrong has still done his job correctly.

**Everything else is decided by two of three.** Deadlock goes to a night's sleep
and a second vote, then to an outside advisor named in advance.

**Once a year, in January, the three of us read this document out loud, in a
room, together.** Not to admire it — to argue with it. Anything we have stopped
doing gets struck or gets fixed. **A constitution nobody has broken is a
constitution nobody has tested.**

---

## Article XI — Amendment

Everything in this document may be amended by unanimous agreement of the
partners, in writing, with the date and the reason recorded — **except Article
VI, value 2, and Article IX, which may not be amended, suspended, or waived by
anybody, for any reason, at any price.**

When you amend something, **write down what you believed at the time.** The
company that comes after us will need to know not just what we changed, but what
we were wrong about.

---

## Article XII — The Charge

Roosevelt's argument, stripped of its century, is simply this: **the reckoning
comes either way, and the only question is whether you meet it having done
something.**

We are not owed this business. Nobody is waiting for us. The market we are
walking into is consolidating, the margins are being competed away, and there are
larger, better-capitalized companies who would not notice if we disappeared.

**Good. That is the only kind of thing worth starting.**

We know how the other version ends, because it is in one of our families. A
company can be brilliant for eighty-three years and still die in a drawer,
because the men who ran it never built the thing that would outlive them.

> **So build the clock.**
>
> Take off the job. Cut it right. Label it. Put it on the deck. Measure the
> steel. Tell the truth about the date. Pay everybody. Go and look. Teach
> somebody else to do it without you.
>
> Then do it again tomorrow.
