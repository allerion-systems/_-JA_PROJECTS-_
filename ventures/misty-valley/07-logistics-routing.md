# Getting It To Bonnieville — Routing Analysis

Joey asked for barge on the Ohio and rail to be considered properly. Both were
researched. Here is what's real.

**Two findings that change what happens next, before any routing decision:**

> **1. A 22-tonne container cannot legally reach Bonnieville.** Kentucky's
> 80,000 lb limit is absolute for divisible loads, there is no permit, and
> there is no port corridor exemption. **Tell the supplier to load 20.0 tonnes
> maximum, before the first booking.**
>
> **2. Long steel bundles do not come out of a 40ft container at a yard with no
> dock.** The container floor sits ~53 inches above grade. This is the most
> commonly under-costed item in an import plan of this type, and none of the
> routes below work until it is solved.

## 2. Barge — the answer is no, and it isn't close

This got the most research because it was the most asked about. The finding is
unambiguous.

**There is no container-on-barge service on the Ohio River to any Kentucky
port.** The largest COB network in the United States runs **New Orleans –
Baton Rouge – Memphis – St. Louis**, operated by Port NOLA, Port of Greater
Baton Rouge and Ingram, moving ~30,000 TEU/year on a weekly service
([Port NOLA](https://portnola.com/info/news-media/press-releases/port-of-new-orleans-container-on-barge-service-sets-new-record)).
**The Ohio River is not on it.** And its purpose is largely repositioning empty
containers upriver and moving resin exports down — it is not an import-delivery
product.

### What Kentucky's river ports can actually do

| Port | Container capability | What it really handles |
|---|---|---|
| **Jefferson Riverport / Port of Louisville** | **No container crane, no COB service.** A 30-ton overhead crane on a general cargo dock | Coal, grain, fertilizer, potash; lumber and steel at the general dock |
| **Owensboro** | No scheduled COB. Has an 84-ton mobile harbour crane — *could* lift a box, but no service, no chassis pool, no stack yard | Metals, paper, bulk, project cargo |
| **Paducah–McCracken** | **The only one that ever tried** | See below |
| **Henderson, Hickman, Eddyville** | No | Bulk, breakbulk, fertilizer, grain |

**The Paducah story is the whole story.** Paducah received a **federal grant in
2020 specifically to obtain the equipment to begin regularly scheduled
container-on-barge service** — a business case anchored on 2,080 TEU/year from
Calvert City chemical shippers who currently truck to Savannah, funded by a
$407,000 Delta Regional Authority grant plus a $251,927 Marine Highway grant
plus state and port money. It won a MARAD Marine Highway Leadership Award.

Six years later, the port's own **$6.6M grant retrospective (Sept 2025)** and
its **$24M BUILD-funded expansion announcement (July 2026)** discuss radial
stackers, ground conveyors, storage domes, truck scales, fertilizer, sand,
limestone and graphite — **and not one word about container operations**
([Waterways Journal](https://www.waterwaysjournal.net/2026/07/10/24-million-grant-advances-paducah-port/)).

**Kentucky's first container-on-barge terminal was announced, funded, awarded,
and did not happen.** The regional freight planners say why plainly: most Ohio
River ports are small, have limited operating capital, and cannot afford the
terminal investment COB requires.

The same pattern holds regionally — **American Patriot Holdings'** hybrid river
containerships, the flagship Plaquemines-to-Memphis COB concept promised for
April 2024, never entered service. The trade press headline is *"Boxed In."*

### Even if it existed, the arithmetic fails

**Transit:** New Orleans → Louisville is **17 days by barge** (Memphis 7,
St. Louis 12). Door to door that is **52–59 days** against 35–42 on Route A.
**You would add roughly three weeks.**

**Volume:** a jumbo hopper barge carries ~1,500 short tons.

| Volume | Months to fill ONE barge |
|---|---:|
| 1–2 containers/month | **34–68 months** |
| 5/month | 13.6 months |
| 10/month | 6.8 months |
| 25/month | 2.7 months |
| **~68/month** | **1 month** |

**To move one barge a month you need about 68 containers a month.** That is
13× the high end of the current plan. And filling one barge monthly is the bare
minimum to be a credible customer, not the point at which barge gets cheap.

**Reliability:** 2026 was a bad river year at both ends of the hydrograph — ACBL
triggered its low-water protocol on the Lower Mississippi on 3 August 2026, and
a Vessel Traffic Service was activated at Louisville on 13 August 2026 for
McAlpine high water.

**The linehaul was never the problem.** At the USDA's late-July 2026 St. Louis
spot of $25.62/ton, 22 tons of studs is **$564** of barge linehaul. The problem
is that there is no crane, no stack yard, no chassis pool and no scheduled
service at the other end — which is exactly what killed Paducah.

### Why it works elsewhere and not here

The **James River "64 Express"** (Port of Virginia) runs 80–100 containers
three times weekly, Hampton Roads to Richmond, in **12 hours**. Note what makes
it work: one port authority on both ends, 100 river miles, gantry cranes at
both terminals, and an interstate bottleneck to route around. **New Orleans to
Louisville has none of those properties.**

> **Verdict: not an option at 1–5/month, at 10/month, or at 25/month.**
> Revisit only if Paducah's Riverport West opens with genuine container
> capability after 2028 **and** volume exceeds ~50 containers/month **and**
> transit time genuinely stops mattering.

### Breakbulk barge — the better question, still no

Shipping the steel loose rather than containerized is a more interesting idea,
and the physical pathway exists (Port NOLA offers direct ship-to-barge
discharge for breakbulk; Owensboro handles steel with a real crane and 500,000
sq ft of indoor warehouse). It fails for a different reason:

- **Handling count explodes** — 2 lifts containerized vs **6–8 touches**
  breakbulk. Bent studs are scrap.
- **Galvanized steel and river transport are a bad match.** White rust forms
  *"when moisture condenses on tightly wound coils and cannot evaporate"* and
  develops where *"the air is highly moisture laden and the ambient air is
  still"* ([Skuld](https://www.skuld.com/topics/cargo/steel/carriage-of-steel-cargoes/)).
  A covered barge on the Ohio in a humid Kentucky summer for 17 days is close
  to a worst case.
- **Ocean breakbulk minimums** are ~1,000+ tons per parcel — 45+
  container-equivalents per shipment.

Worth re-examining only at 25+ containers/month, and then as ocean breakbulk to
a port with a warehouse and a truck — **not as a river move to a Kentucky
riverport.**

## 3. Rail — real, and the right long-run answer

Both railroads handle international boxes into Louisville:

- **CSX** — 34-acre intermodal terminal at 8021 National Turnpike, on I-65/I-264
  south of the airport. Handles domestic *and international* containers, serves
  23 destinations including Savannah. **62 miles from Bonnieville** — about as
  good as ramp positioning gets.
- **Norfolk Southern** — restructured effective **26 January 2026**: **Buechel
  reopened specifically to handle international shipments** (Port of Virginia
  boxes ground there), West Coast international grounds at Appliance Park,
  **70 miles out**.

**The catch is transit.** SC Ports' published intermodal schedule quotes
**CSX Charleston → Louisville at 6 days** — six days of rail to cover a
distance a truck covers in eleven hours. You trade **~5 days of transit for
~$400 of inland cost.**

**One caution:** NS closed Buechel in summer 2023, reopened it that October,
and restructured it again in January 2026. **Do not build a single-ramp
dependency on it.**

**The structural trend favours rail.** 2026 rail increases are running low
single digits (committed pricing +2–5% YoY) while **DAT truckload spot is
+42.4% YoY.** The gap is widening in rail's favour.

## 4. Kentucky will not permit you out of the weight problem

**This is the finding that would have stopped the first container.**

Kentucky's maximum gross vehicle weight is **80,000 lb**
([603 KAR 5:066](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/titles/603/005/066/)).
And under
[601 KAR 1:018](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/titles/601/001/018/),
*"An overweight or overdimensional permit shall not be issued for a divisible
load."* KYTC also denies applications where the route includes any part of the
Interstate system and the load is divisible.

**A container of steel studs is a divisible load and the route is I-65. There
is no permit.**

The 120,000 lb figure people cite is the **Extended Weight Coal Haul Road
System** (KRS 177.9771) — coal and coal by-products only, on certified routes.
**Steel studs are not coal.** Industry references list Kentucky flatly as
*"will not permit"* for overweight containers.

```
Legal GVW                                80,000 lb
  less day-cab tractor                  −17,000 lb
  less 40ft tandem chassis              − 7,000 lb
  less empty container tare             − 8,300 lb
                                        -----------
Theoretical maximum cargo                47,700 lb = 21.6 t
```

**But axle limits bite before the gross limit does.** With dense steel on a
fixed-spread chassis, the tandem group and bridge formula cap practical payload
at **44,000–45,000 lb (20.0–20.4 tonnes)**.

> **Instruct the supplier: maximum 20.0 tonnes (44,090 lb) per 40ft container,
> evenly distributed fore and aft.** Loading 20 t rather than 22 t raises
> freight cost per tonne by 10% — but that penalty applies identically to every
> route, so it does not change the ranking. It just has to be budgeted.

## 5. The no-dock problem — solve this before routing

A 40ft container floor sits **~53 inches above grade** on a standard chassis.
Cold-formed framing arrives in long bundles that are unloaded **from the side
by forklift** — which is how you unload a flatbed and precisely not how you
unload a container.

| Option | Cost | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Portable dock ramp, or tilt-bed / roll-off chassis | ~$250/move, or $15–35k to own | Included as "ground-level delivery" in §1 |
| Side-loader chassis to ground the box in the yard | $300–500/move | Frees the truck; you unload at leisure |
| **Transload to flatbed** | $300–600 + $100 tarps | Delivers in the form the yard can actually receive |

**Transload to flatbed — not to a 53ft dry van.** Steel *weighs out*, it does
not cube out, so the classic 3-containers-into-2-trailers consolidation does
not work here:

```
3 containers × 20.0 t          = 60.0 t
Legal payload per 53ft van     ≈ 20.4 t
60.0 ÷ 20.4                    = 2.94 trailers  — you save 0.06 of a truck
```

**But flatbed transload is worth it, for a different reason:**

```
Savannah dray to transload facility          $  575
Transload 40ft floor-loaded → flatbed        $  450
Tarps                                        $  100
Flatbed linehaul + fuel, 607 mi              $2,140
                                             ------
Total transloaded                            $3,265
vs. direct container dray + ground equipment $3,150
                                             ------
Delta                                        + $115
```

**$115 per container buys out the entire per-diem and detention tail risk, and
solves the dock problem outright.** Against a $150–600 accessorial exposure per
box, that is clearly positive. A no-dock yard unloading long bundles with a
ramp will not clear a container in two hours, and detention runs $75–200/day
with chassis at $40/day on top.

## 6. What the import has to beat

**ClarkDietrich's West Chester, Ohio plant is ~180 miles from Bonnieville.**

```
Flatbed linehaul $2.90/mi × 1.19 short-haul = $3.45/mi × 180  = $621
Fuel $0.725/mi × 180                                          = $131
Tarping                                                       = $100
                                                                ----
TOTAL PER TRUCKLOAD                                             $852
```

| | Domestic (West Chester OH) | Import (Route A, spot) | Import (normalized) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Freight per 20-tonne load | **$852** | **$13,675** | **$8,075** |
| **Freight per tonne** | **$43** | **$684** | **$404** |
| Transit, order to yard | **1–2 days** | 35–42 days | 35–42 days |
| Working capital in transit | ~nil | 5–6 weeks | 5–6 weeks |
| Minimum order | 1 truckload | 1 container | 1 container |

**The freight delta is $640/tonne today and $360/tonne in a normal ocean
market.** For the import to work, delivered Chinese steel must beat the Ohio
mill price by more than that — **after the 75% duty stack in `02`.**

And note the strategic point that falls out of this: **at $43/tonne of freight
and 1–2 day lead times, domestic sourcing is not the fallback — it is the
structurally advantaged option for a business whose product is delivery in
sequence.** You cannot run a responsive cut shop off a 40-day pipeline.

## 7. Recommendation by volume

**1–2 containers/month → Route A.** Savannah or Charleston plus a single truck.
At 12–24 boxes a year there is no pricing leverage anywhere; Route B's $400
saving costs 5–6 days and adds a rail cutoff, ramp dwell, and a second party to
chase. Buy a ramp or a tilt chassis. Negotiate **5 days free time minimum**.
Book port-to-door through one NVOCC so per-diem is their problem.
**Do not transload, chase the West Coast, or call a barge line.**

**5/month → Route A primary, qualify Route B in parallel.** Put ~70% on truck
for schedule certainty and ~30% on rail to build a service record and earn a
quote. Start pricing the Savannah flatbed transload.

**10+/month → Route B primary, Route A as the expedite lane.** 120+ boxes a
year earns committed intermodal pricing, and the rail/truck gap is widening.
Move to a **Louisville transload** — it converts the awkward container delivery
into a flatbed the yard can handle, and creates a buffer inventory position.

**25+/month → Route B with committed rail plus a Louisville deconsolidation
position.** At this tier the inventory buffer that decouples a 40-day ocean
pipeline from weekly cut-shop demand is worth far more than the last $300 of
freight optimisation. Contract with both railroads so one ramp closure does not
stop you.

**West Coast (Route C) is a switch, not a strategy.** It wins only while the
East–West ocean spread exceeds **~$1,950/FEU**. Today it is $2,170–$2,402, so
it wins by a little. In May 2026 the spread was $1,000 and it lost by ~$950.
**Check monthly; do not institutionalise it.**

---

## 8. The three things to act on

1. **Cap containers at 20.0 tonnes.** Before the first booking. There is no
   permit and no workaround.
2. **Solve the no-dock problem before the routing problem.** Buy the ground
   handling equipment or commit to a flatbed transload. Every route above
   assumes one or the other, and none work without it.
3. **Price the duty first.** At roughly $8,250 per container on an $11,000
   invoice (`02`), the tariff is **more than ten times** the entire spread
   between the best and worst routing option. This is a tariff decision wearing
   a freight costume.
