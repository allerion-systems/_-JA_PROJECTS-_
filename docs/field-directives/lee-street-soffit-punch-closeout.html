01

What is actually wrong

The soffit panels are not the problem. The line they die into is.

The framers cut the canopy beam in the field with a chainsaw and a Sawzall, so the face the splice trim had to land on is not straight and not in one plane. When the installers ran the two panel fields, they had a choice: follow the crooked beam and let the red panel runs go out of square with the building, or hold the panels square and absorb the error at the trim. They held the panels square and opened the joint 1/16″ to 1/8″ to do it.

That was the right call structurally and the wrong call visually. A red field against a bright white line is the highest-contrast edge on the whole canopy, and the eye reads that white line as a straightedge. Any wander in the reveal — and the wander here changes along the run and steps at the trim's own butt splice — reads as sloppy work even though the panels themselves are dead square.

Why we are not stripping it

Tearing the fields off does not fix a crooked beam. You would re-hang the same panels against the same substrate and land in the same place, plus a week of schedule and a canopy full of new fastener holes. The defect lives in the substrate, so the fix has to be a piece of metal that spans it.

02

PL‑01 — The reverse‑J cover cap

Brake a shallow channel out of the same white coil the existing trim came from, lap it over the whole joint, and rivet it off. The cap's own edges become the straight line the eye was looking for; the wander disappears underneath the flange.

Set the cap to a string, not to the trim

This is the step that decides whether the fix works. The existing trim line is the crooked thing you are hiding — if you set the cap by holding it to that trim, you will build a second crooked line one inch wider and the client will reject it again. Pull a dry line end to end, set the cap edge to the string, and let the flange width eat the difference.

Field‑measured 25 Aug — supersedes the estimates below

The soffit assembly measures 1‑1/8″, so the channel is ordered at 1‑1/8″ inside dimension to slip up over it. Order placed with Superior Metal: 24 sticks at 10′‑0″, 240 LF. Leg depths, exposed face width, gauge and coating still to be confirmed with the shop before they brake it.

Nobody sells this part — and the no‑fastener version costs more than it looks

Worth knowing before anyone goes shopping: there is no stock trim for this condition. Every “cover” product on the market is either a batten cap that runs parallel to the ribs, a parapet coping, or a new‑install splice detail that needs the panel ends cut into a receiver. Berridge’s 2″ cap is the widest stock cover face out there, and 2″ barely laps a joint that already has slop in it. This gets braked.

The tempting alternative is a two‑piece cover — a fastened backer with a cap that snaps over it and hides every fastener. That is real manufacturer practice, not an improvisation. But the snap in those systems is a positive hook‑and‑rotate interlock over formed kicks on both pieces, not a friction fit, so it means forming two profiles instead of one. And the manufacturers who ship them still require mechanical or adhesive securement on top of the snap, because the cap moves thermally. If you go that way the backer must be continuous and shimmed to the string — a segmented cleat cannot hold a line straight over a crooked beam. For a Wednesday, the riveted one‑piece is the right call; keep the two‑piece in your pocket for the next one.

Do not substitute caulk

Running a bead of white sealant in the reveal is tempting and it will look acceptable for about two months. Then it shrinks, picks up dirt, and yellows against a red field on a south‑facing canopy. The client already rejected living with it; caulk is living with it with extra steps and a second callback.

Install sequence

  1. Close PL‑02 first. Every loose panel gets refastened before anything gets measured. You cannot size a cap against a soffit that is still moving.
  2. Measure the worst case. Five points minimum along the run, edge of existing trim to the far side of the widest gap. Take the largest number and size the whole run off it — one cap width for the whole canopy.
  3. Confirm the color. Chip against the existing trim outdoors, not under the cans. Do this before the brake runs.
  4. Brake and dry‑fit one piece. Hang a single length, look at it from the sidewalk at the angle the client looks at it. Adjust before you make the other hundred feet.
  5. Pull the string. End to end, both edges. Snap a chalk line on the panel faces if the run is long enough to sag a string.
  6. Pack the hollows. Where the substrate falls away behind the cap, shim it so the face does not dish when the rivets pull.
  7. Set to the string and rivet off. Gauge every rivet the same distance from the edge. Work one direction so the laps all face the same way.
  8. Close the ends, then photograph. Same angles as the rejection photos, so the before-and-after reads at a glance in the closeout package.
03

PL‑02 — White soffit pieces hanging below the line

Straightforward rework, but find out why each piece is loose before refastening — putting another screw through a panel that has nothing behind it just makes a second hole.

  • No backing. Add blocking to the framing, then fasten to the blocking. Most common cause on a field‑cut canopy.
  • Fastener missed the framing. Locate the member, refasten, and fill or replace the piece if the miss left a visible hole in the finish face.
  • Receiver leg is too shallow to hold the panel. Add a concealed cleat or Z‑closure fastened to framing and hook the panel to it. Do not try to fix a shallow receiver with more fasteners.

Refasten with color‑matched pancake‑head or stitch screws in the panel flats, and keep the fastener line consistent for the same reason the rivet line has to be consistent. This item has to be complete before the cap gets measured.

04

PL‑03 — The fascia scope question

The client believes the fascia was bought. The installer has it flagged as different scope. Nobody is going to win this one across a table, so take it off the table and onto paper — in this order, because each step is stronger evidence than the one before it.

  1. The subcontract scope exhibit. Read the inclusions and the exclusions. Most of these end here.
  2. The bid takeoff and the material PO. If fascia coil and trim were quantified and purchased, it was in the number, and that is very hard to walk back.
  3. The drawings and the finish schedule. Exterior elevations plus the spec section referenced in the sub — who is carrying the fascia finish.
  4. The approved shop drawings. If fascia was shown and came back approved without comment, that is a strong argument it is in.
The move that keeps the GC on your side

If it lands in scope: do it, say nothing else about it, and let the goodwill count for something.

If it lands out of scope: write a short clarification citing the exact exhibit paragraph, price it as a PCO — and offer to install it during Wednesday's mobilization anyway, with the change order reconciled after. Buffalo gets a finished building on schedule, the money conversation happens on paper, and you save a second trip that would have cost more than the argument is worth.

What you never do is let a scope dispute hold up a punch item that is yours. That is the thing that actually burns a GC relationship, and it is the reason patience is running short here.

One thing not to claim

Do not tell Buffalo this remedy “meets industry standard.” We went looking for a published acceptance tolerance for reveal and alignment in architectural metal soffit work — MCA, SMACNA, NRCA, AAMA, a real 07 42 13 section — and nothing citable came back. The 1″ lap and the string‑line criterion are sound engineering judgment, and they are defensible as such, but they are not a standard you can point at. The one figure that does trace to a published standard is the 1/2″ hem.

Say it as judgment and it holds up. Dress it as a code citation and the first person who looks it up wins the argument.

Three things in that message do the work: a root cause that is not an excuse, a dated commitment with a time on it, and an offer on the disputed item that removes the schedule risk from the GC's plate.
