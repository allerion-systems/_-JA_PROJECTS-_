Part 2

Build 1 — the front room curtain wall

Opening measures 60″ wide × ~82″ tall — too wide for one door, perfect for a curtain wall. Zero holes, 20-minute install.

  1. Heavy-duty tension rod rated for ~36–66″ — at 60″ even a mid-grade rod won't sag.
  2. Two standard 52″ × 84″ blackout panels: 104″ of fabric over a 60″ gap is ~1.7× fullness, so it drapes like a wall instead of stretching like a bedsheet.
  3. Mount the rod ~1″ below the top of the casing — 84″ panels just kiss the floor, no hemming.
  4. Velcro dots or magnets where the panels meet; one Command hook per side to tie panels back during the day.

The bed's already in there: the pullout couch has a queen inside — pull it out, topper + white bedding, done. $0. Placement check: a queen pullout needs ~7 ft of clear floor when open — orient the couch so it opens away from the curtain line, keep the console table as the nightstand, luggage stools on the front-door side.

Front-door logistics: that room is the entryway. Sign on the front door, everyone uses the side/back door all weekend, and frost film or a mini-curtain over the door's fan window.

Upgrade path: 60″ = exactly two 30″ bifold or French doors (~$130) if the Airbnb era wants a hard door later. Curtain first.

Prehung = easiest install there is: the door comes already hung in its jamb — tip the unit into the RO, shim behind the hinges, screw through the jamb, ~30–45 min each. No hinge mortising, no fitting.
  • Studs 24" OC, king + jack each side, flat headers, no cripples. Both doors open onto the middle lounge — BR-B's in its 8-ft north wall, BR-A's in its 11-ft south wall near the corridor side. The lounge is the circulation.

Part 4

The modular wall upgrade — post-wedding, not wedding-budget

For the wedding, the lean build is curtain walls. When Airbnb money is real, these are the upgrade paths. Commercial temp offices, NYC pressurized walls, and the demountable partitions the government buys all share one anatomy: floor track + vertical posts + infill panels, held by compression instead of screws into the building, gasketed so they demount without a trace, and stopped short of the ceiling for airflow:

A · Tension-pole walls ~$80–120 per wall · zero holes · zero wood

  • Floor-to-ceiling spring-tension divider poles every 4–5 ft, top rail between them.
  • Infill: blackout fabric, or clip white corrugated-plastic panels to the rail for a solid-wall look.
  • Best for: quick pods on floors with solid ceilings (mid level, front room upgrades). Not for the basement — drop ceilings can't take compression.

B · Steel-stud + frosted polycarbonate — the no-wood alternative ~$180–220 per 12-ft wall

  • Drop ceilings can't take compression — so this wall is end-anchored, not pressure-fit: the 12-ft run terminates into the existing side walls with 2 screws into studs at each end. Four small, patchable holes — total damage.
  • 25-gauge steel track top and bottom, steel studs every 24″ — cut with aviation snips, assembled with pan-head screws. No saw, no sawdust.
  • Frame stops 4–6″ below the ceiling grid: HVAC keeps flowing, nothing touches the tiles.
  • Skin with twin-wall polycarbonate screwed to the studs with washers — the skin stiffens the wall (same job drywall does commercially) and glows like a frosted-glass office wall.
  • Leave one 36″ bay as the doorway with a double-layer curtain inside the frame; upgrade later to a polycarb slider hung from the wall's own top track — never from the ceiling.
  • Demounts clean at lease-end; panels unscrew and reuse. The flagship of the future Airbnb photos.

C · Used cubicle panels ~$10–40 per panel used · literally the government system

  • The modular partitions federal offices actually buy (Steelcase, Herman Miller) hit liquidators and Facebook Marketplace constantly when offices close.
  • Freestanding, acoustic-padded, walls in an hour, no build at all.
  • Best for: grab a cheap local set whenever one appears — it beats building.

Rules the pros follow — copy these too

  • Stop walls 6–12″ short of the ceiling: HVAC keeps flowing and nothing feels like a submarine.
  • Never block the path to stairs or exits.
  • $15 battery smoke alarm inside every partitioned sleeping space.
  • Nothing within 3 ft of the wood stove. Landlord heads-up before anything that looks structural.
  • The 60″ front-room opening stays a curtain either way — a doorway used fifty times a day wants fabric, not panels.

Part 7

The perfect-guest-experience playbook

Five couples in one house works when nobody has to ask for anything. Every item here kills a question before it gets asked.

Arrival

  • Text everyone a mini house map before they drive in: which door to use, which floor is theirs, where to park.
  • Name card on each room/pod — it makes a curtained corner feel official instead of improvised.
  • Towel set per couple, folded on their bed. Wifi card, water bottles, snack basket per room.

Mornings — the real bottleneck

  • Eleven people, two showers — back-door entry level + upstairs full bath. Post a loose order on each door: lower floors use the entry-level shower, bedroom floor uses upstairs. Wedding morning is bride-first upstairs, non-negotiable.
  • Coffee station on the kitchen level the night before: machine loaded, mugs out, creamer visible. First riser pushes one button.
  • Each bathroom stocked: spare toilet paper where guests can see it, hand towels, a plunger nobody has to ask for.

Nights

  • The mid-level lounge is the sanctioned afterparty room — that's a feature. White-noise fans in both fabric pods make it work.
  • Lamp-only light after 10pm on sleeping floors; the overheads stay off.
  • Phone charger + power strip within reach of every single bed. This is the cheapest five-star review there is.

After — the Airbnb debrief

  • White duvet covers everywhere — looks hotel-clean, bleaches back to new.
  • Ask every couple for one honest gripe afterward — that's your review system, and it's more honest than real reviews.
  • Photograph every staged room before guests arrive: future listing gallery, free.
Part 8

Yard & shed system — where the mower goes

The 10×10 storage room is full and off-limits, so the yard gear moves outside — which also gets fuel and engine fumes off a floor that's about to be a bedroom.

7×4 shed (the new one) — machine bay

  • Mower parked nose-in along the 7-ft axis, handle folded, one side of the door.
  • Power washer in the opposite front corner, hose coiled on a wall hook beside it.
  • Rail-and-hook kit on the back wall: shovels, rakes, trimmer hung vertically — floor stays drivable for the mower.
  • Small shelf up top: oil, trimmer string, gloves.

3×5 metal shed (assemble it) — fuel + chemicals

  • Set it on 2–4 concrete pavers for a level, dry, rust-free base.
  • Gas cans, premix, fertilizer, garden chemicals — separated from the machines and the house.
  • House rule: no fuel and no gas engines stored indoors, ever. That basement is a bedroom now.

Wedding bonus: a cleared yard and tidy sheds means the outside of the house is photo-ready the same weekend the inside is.
